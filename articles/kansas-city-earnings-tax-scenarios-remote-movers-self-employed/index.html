Do You Owe the Kansas City Earnings Tax? Eight Real-Life Scenarios

The 1% earnings tax is simple on paper and confusing in real life. These scenarios cover the questions I hear most.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • The Kansas City, Missouri earnings tax is 1% and generally applies to residents on all earnings and to non-residents on earnings for work done in the city.
  • Where you do the work usually matters more than where your employer is located.
  • Self-employed people owe tax on net profit, and there is no employer withholding to handle it for you.
  • Keep a day-by-day log if you split time between the city and elsewhere.
  • These are general rules. Confirm your situation with the Kansas City, Missouri Revenue Division.

Kansas City, Missouri has a 1% earnings tax that sits on top of your federal and state taxes. It is one of those taxes that surprises people who move here, commute here or start working from a spare room. I will not pretend every edge case has a single answer. What follows are eight scenarios, answered in general terms, followed by how to confirm yours.

The basic framework

Three ideas explain most cases. First, residents of the city are generally taxed on their earnings wherever they work. Second, non-residents are generally taxed only on earnings for work performed inside the city. Third, the tax applies to earned income, meaning wages and net profit from self-employment, rather than investment income, Social Security or typical retirement distributions. The tax is separate from Kansas City, Kansas, and from suburbs such as Overland Park or Lee's Summit, which do not have the same tax.

Scenario 1: I live in Kansas City, Missouri and work remotely for an employer in another state

As a resident, your earnings are generally subject to the tax even though the company is elsewhere. Out-of-state employers often do not withhold it, so you may need to file and pay yourself. Do not assume that no withholding means no tax.

Scenario 2: I live in Overland Park and work remotely for a Kansas City, Missouri employer

Because you are a non-resident, the question is where the work is physically performed. Days worked from your home in Kansas are generally not earnings from work in the city, but your employer may be withholding on everything by default. If so, ask how the employer handles remote days and whether an adjustment or refund applies. Ask in writing and keep the answer.

Scenario 3: I moved into the city in July

Residency is generally measured by period. Earnings while you lived in the city are taxed as a resident. Before the move, you are taxed as a non-resident, which means only on work performed in the city. Keep your lease or closing date and a payroll summary that separates the periods.

Scenario 4: I moved out in March

The mirror image. Until the move you are a resident, and after it you are taxed only on city-based work. If you commute into the city, that part continues to be taxed.

Scenario 5: I am self-employed and my home office is in Kansas City, Missouri

If you live and work in the city, net profit from your business is generally subject to the tax. Net profit is your income minus allowable expenses, so good records reduce the base. You are responsible for filing and paying, since no employer withholds. If this is your first year as a freelancer, my estimated tax calendar for a first-year freelancer shows how to fit payments like this into your yearly routine.

Scenario 6: I live in Kansas but my clients are in Kansas City, Missouri

This depends on where you perform the services. If you do the work at your home in Kansas, the city generally does not treat that profit as earned in the city. If you perform services at client sites in the city, the profit tied to that work may be taxable. Track days and locations.

Scenario 7: I split my week between a downtown office and home elsewhere

Allocation is the answer. Many people use a day count: city workdays divided by total workdays. A calendar, building access logs or timesheets support the numbers. A guess made in March of the next year is weak support. A running log is strong support.

Scenario 8: I have a W-2 job and a side business

Both streams can be subject to the tax if they fall under the rules above. Withholding on the W-2 job does not cover the business profit. Keep the business income and expenses separate, the way I describe in my piece on bookkeeping software or a bookkeeper.

How to confirm your own answer

  1. Write down where you lived each month of the year.
  2. Write down where you physically worked, by day if it varies.
  3. Gather pay stubs showing any city withholding.
  4. Contact the Kansas City, Missouri Revenue Division for current forms, rules and filing methods. The city can change instructions and deadlines.
  5. Bring the records to your preparer so the city return and the state return agree. I list the items worth checking in my piece on what a preparer checks before signing your return.

A common mistake is ignoring the tax because no one withheld it. The second most common is paying it on wages that were not city earnings. Both are fixable with a simple record of where you lived and worked. Learn more about my background on the about me page.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Kansas City earnings tax apply to Kansas City, Kansas?

No. The 1% earnings tax is a Kansas City, Missouri tax. Kansas City, Kansas and other Kansas communities have different local rules.

Is investment or retirement income subject to the earnings tax?

Generally no. The tax applies to earned income, meaning wages and net profit from self-employment, rather than interest, dividends, Social Security or typical retirement distributions.

If my employer withholds the city tax, am I done?

Often yes for those wages, but check whether the withholding matches where you lived and worked. Business profit or work done outside the city may need separate treatment.

Can I deduct business expenses from the city tax?

For self-employed people, the base is generally net profit, so ordinary business expenses reduce it. Rules for specific deductions can differ from federal rules, so confirm with the Revenue Division.

Where do I get official answers?

The Kansas City, Missouri Revenue Division is the authority for forms, rules and deadlines. Use it to confirm anything specific to your case.
