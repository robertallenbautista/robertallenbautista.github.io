IRS Payment Plans Explained: Short-Term vs Installment Agreement, Fees and Who Qualifies

The common questions when the balance is more than the bank account, answered from the IRS payment plan pages.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator, CPA candidate · Published 2026-11-19

Key takeaways

  • Short-term plan: 180 days or less, no setup fee listed by the IRS.
  • Long-term plan (installment agreement): online for eligible individuals who owe $50,000 or less and have filed all required returns.
  • Setup fees depend on how you pay and how you apply, and low-income taxpayers may get a waiver or reimbursement.
  • Interest keeps running on any plan, so the faster you pay it off the less it costs.

Pay in full within 180 days and the IRS lists no setup fee for the plan. Need longer, and the cost changes. If you owe and cannot pay, the vocabulary comes first: what is a payment plan, what is an installment agreement, are they the same thing, and what will it cost? The answers are below. Every number comes from the IRS payment plan and penalty pages, and those pages change, so confirm the current figures on IRS.gov before you apply.

What is the difference between the two plan types?

The IRS describes two kinds. A short-term payment plan is for people who can pay the full amount in 180 days or less. A long-term payment plan, also called an installment agreement, is for people who need more than 180 days and will make monthly payments. In both cases the IRS says interest and some penalty charges continue to be added until the balance is paid in full.

Who can apply online?

Per the IRS, individuals who owe less than $100,000 in combined tax, penalties and interest can request a short-term plan online. For a long-term plan, individuals who owe $50,000 or less in combined tax, penalties and interest and who have filed all required returns can apply online. If your balance is above those lines, the IRS still has ways to apply, but they may involve more paperwork and a conversation with a collection employee.

What does it cost to set up?

The IRS lists no setup fee for a short-term plan. For a long-term plan, the listed setup fees are:

  • Direct debit: $29 if you apply online, $107 if you apply by phone, mail or in person.
  • Other payment methods: $69 online, $178 by phone, mail or in person.
  • Changing an existing plan: $6 online, $89 by phone, mail or in person. The IRS lists $0 for changes to an existing direct debit plan.

The pattern is clear: apply online and use direct debit if you can. It is the cheapest route, and the automatic payment also means you will not miss a due date.

Is there a break for low income?

Yes. The IRS says people with adjusted gross income at or below 250% of the federal poverty level who enter a long-term plan may get the setup fee waived if they use direct debit. Without direct debit, the fee may be reimbursed after the agreement is complete. To request reconsideration of the fee, the IRS says to submit Form 13844 within 30 days of the acceptance letter. Check the poverty guidelines for your household size before assuming you do or do not qualify.

How do I apply?

You can apply through your IRS online account, or mail Form 9465, Installment Agreement Request. The IRS also lists a phone option for people who do not want to apply online. Before applying, gather your return, the notice if you have one, your bank details if you want direct debit, and a realistic monthly budget. If you are missing documents from the tax year, my tax season document checklist covers what to collect.

Do penalties stop when I am on a plan?

No, but one penalty can shrink. The IRS failure to pay page says the penalty is normally 0.5% of the unpaid tax per month or part of a month, up to 25%. For individuals who filed on time and have an approved payment plan, the rate drops to 0.25% per month. Interest continues, and per the IRS interest page it accrues daily and the rate can change each quarter. I will not quote the rate here because it moves. Check it on IRS.gov.

What happens if I miss a payment?

I cannot tell you what happens in your case, but I can tell you what to avoid. A plan is an agreement, and the rules include filing and paying future taxes on time. If you owe because of underwithholding on a job, a quick fix is to adjust it so you are not building a new balance. My article on the paycheck withholding checkup shows how to use the IRS estimator. If your income is irregular and you are self-employed, quarterly estimates do the same job.

When is a plan not the right tool?

If paying anything would leave you short on basic living costs, ask the IRS about temporarily delaying collection. The IRS says interest and penalties keep accruing in that status and a lien may be filed. If the debt is so large that you could never pay it in full, an offer in compromise may be worth testing with the IRS Pre-Qualifier tool. The IRS generally will not accept an offer if you can pay in full through a plan, so the plan is usually the first question to settle.

The short version

Know your balance, file all returns, pick the lightest plan that fits, apply online with direct debit if possible and read the letters that follow. State taxes in Missouri and Kansas have their own payment arrangements, so ask those revenue departments separately.

Frequently asked questions

How long is a short-term payment plan?

The IRS describes a short-term plan as paying the amount owed in 180 days or less. The IRS lists no setup fee, and interest and penalties continue until the balance is paid.

What are the setup fees for an installment agreement?

The IRS lists $29 online and $107 by phone, mail or in person with direct debit, and $69 online and $178 by phone, mail or in person with other payment methods.

Can I get the setup fee waived?

The IRS says taxpayers at or below 250% of the federal poverty level may qualify for a waiver with direct debit, or reimbursement without it.

Does a payment plan lower penalties?

Yes, in one case. The IRS says the failure to pay rate drops from 0.5% to 0.25% per month for individuals who filed on time and have an approved plan. Interest continues.
