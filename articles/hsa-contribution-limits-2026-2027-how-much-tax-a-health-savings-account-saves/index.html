HSA Contribution Limits for 2026 and 2027: How Much Tax a Health Savings Account Can Save You

Premiums keep climbing. An HSA is one of the few tools that turns a higher deductible into a tax break. Here are the numbers and the rules.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-20

Key takeaways

  • 2026 HSA limits: $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, plus $1,000 if you are 55 or older.
  • 2027 limits, already announced by the IRS: $4,500 self-only and $9,000 family.
  • An HSA can be tax-free three ways: contributions are deductible or pre-tax, growth is untaxed, and withdrawals for qualified medical costs are tax-free.
  • Since 2026, Exchange bronze and catastrophic plans count as HSA-eligible, certain direct primary care memberships no longer block an HSA, and telehealth before the deductible is permanently allowed.
  • Contributions made through payroll under a cafeteria plan also skip Social Security and Medicare tax. Contributions you make on your own do not.

Health costs rarely go down, and your paycheck feels it. KFF's 2026 Employer Health Benefits Survey puts the average annual premium for employer family coverage at $28,303, up 5% from 2025. Workers paid an average of $7,471 of that, up 9%. A Health Savings Account will not lower the bill. What it can do is change how much of your own money the tax system takes while you pay it.

The rising-cost backdrop

According to KFF's release of October 22, 2026, total family premiums averaged $28,303 this year, and workers with a deductible on single coverage faced an average of $1,955. Those are employer-plan averages, so your own numbers will differ. They do explain why so many people are looking again at high deductible plans, which carry lower premiums in exchange for more cost sharing, and at the account that is designed to go with them.

2026 and 2027 limits at a glance

Every figure below comes from IRS guidance. The 2026 amounts are in Notice 2026-5, which restates Rev. Proc. 2025-19. The 2027 amounts are in Rev. Proc. 2026-24.

  • Contribution limit, self-only coverage: $4,400 in 2026, $4,500 in 2027.
  • Contribution limit, family coverage: $8,750 in 2026, $9,000 in 2027.
  • Catch-up contribution, age 55 or older: an extra $1,000, per the Form 8889 instructions. Rev. Proc. 2026-24 does not list it among the 2027 inflation-adjusted amounts, and it remains $1,000.
  • Minimum deductible for a high deductible health plan (HDHP): $1,700 self-only and $3,400 family in 2026; $1,750 and $3,500 in 2027.
  • Maximum out-of-pocket for an HDHP: $8,500 self-only and $17,000 family in 2026; $8,700 and $17,400 in 2027.

The limit includes everything that goes in, so an employer's contribution counts against your cap. The Form 8889 instructions treat salary-reduction contributions made through a cafeteria plan as employer contributions, and they show up in box 12 of your W-2 with code W. You can read how to find that on my W-2 box-by-box guide.

Who can contribute

You generally need coverage under an HDHP and no other disqualifying coverage, as IRS Notice 2026-5 puts it. A few other rules trip people up:

  • Medicare. Beginning with the first month you are enrolled in Medicare, your contribution limit is zero.
  • The last-month rule. If you are eligible on December 1, you can be treated as eligible all year and contribute the full amount. A testing period then runs through the end of the following year, and failing it generally means including the extra amount in income plus a 10% additional tax.
  • Family versus self-only. The family limit applies when your HDHP covers more than just you.

The three tax advantages

The IRS describes the account's tax treatment in Publication 969.

  1. Contributions. They are deductible on your return whether or not you itemize. Employer contributions are not included in your income.
  2. Growth. Earnings on the money are not included in your income while they sit in the account.
  3. Withdrawals. Amounts used exclusively for qualified medical expenses come out tax-free.

Here is an illustration, not a prediction. If you contributed the full $4,400 self-only limit in 2026 and were in the 22% federal bracket, the federal income tax effect would be $4,400 × 22% = $968. Your bracket may be different, so substitute your own rate.

The payroll trick: skipping FICA too

How the money gets in matters. When your employer runs HSA contributions through a cafeteria plan, they are treated as employer contributions, and IRS Publication 15-B says cash employer contributions to a qualified individual's HSA are exempt from income tax withholding, Social Security tax and Medicare tax, as long as the employer reasonably believes the employee can exclude them. The employee's share of those two payroll taxes is 6.2% plus 1.45%, or 7.65%, per IRS Topic 751.

On the same hypothetical $4,400, 7.65% is $336.60 of payroll tax that never gets withheld. When you instead deposit money into the HSA yourself, you still get the income tax deduction, but practitioner guidance such as this Thomson Reuters explainer is clear that the payroll tax saving applies to payroll contributions through a cafeteria plan, not to direct deposits. If your employer offers payroll HSA contributions, that is usually the cleaner path.

What changed in 2026

The One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21) widened HSA eligibility, and Notice 2026-5 explains how.

Bronze and catastrophic plans

For months beginning after December 31, 2025, a bronze or catastrophic plan available as individual coverage through an Exchange is treated as an HDHP, even if its deductible or out-of-pocket maximum would not otherwise meet the HDHP tests. Notice 2026-5 adds that a plan bought off-Exchange qualifies if the same plan is also offered through an Exchange, and that buying one with an employer's individual coverage HRA does not disqualify it. Bronze plans offered through the small-employer SHOP program generally do not get this treatment. Catastrophic plans have their own enrollment limits, generally under age 30 or a hardship exemption.

Direct primary care memberships

A direct primary care arrangement, meaning primary care services for a fixed periodic fee, no longer makes you ineligible so long as total monthly fees stay at or below $150 for one person or $300 when the arrangement covers more than one person. Those caps are adjusted for inflation after 2026, and Rev. Proc. 2026-24 keeps them at $150 and $300 for 2027. HSA money can also be used to pay the fees.

Telehealth

The safe harbor that lets a plan cover telehealth and other remote care before the deductible without hurting HSA eligibility is now permanent, applying to plan years beginning after December 31, 2024. It does not stretch to in-person care, equipment or drugs that go along with a telehealth visit.

Using it later: the age-65 rule

Take money out for something other than qualified medical expenses before 65 and it is taxable, with an additional 20% tax on top. The IRS says the 20% does not apply to distributions made after you reach 65, become disabled or die, although a non-medical withdrawal is still taxable income. That makes the account a flexible fund for medical costs now and later, with the tax cost of using it for something else far lower after 65.

Deadlines and reporting

You can contribute for a tax year up to the filing deadline. For 2025 the IRS says contributions can be made through April 15, 2026, so for 2026 the usual date would be April 15, 2027. Report contributions and distributions on Form 8889, filed with your Form 1040. The IRS says you must file it if you or anyone else contributed to your HSA, or if the account paid out any money.

Your state may differ

Federal treatment is not automatic everywhere. California does not conform to the federal HSA rules, according to the Franchise Tax Board's Schedule CA guidance, and New Jersey is generally reported to be the other state that does not. If you file in Kansas, Missouri or any other state, check the current state instructions or ask a preparer before assuming they match the federal return.

A short decision checklist

  • Check whether your plan is a qualifying HDHP, or a marketplace bronze or catastrophic plan.
  • Find out whether your employer routes HSA money through a cafeteria plan.
  • Compare the plan's deductible and out-of-pocket maximum with the cash you could really cover in a bad year.
  • Decide whether you can contribute the full amount, and whether you are 55 or older.
  • Keep your receipts. Qualified expenses are what keep withdrawals tax-free.

Frequently asked questions

What is the HSA contribution limit for 2026?

For 2026 the limit is $4,400 with self-only high deductible health plan coverage and $8,750 with family coverage, per IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-19. People age 55 or older can add a $1,000 catch-up contribution.

What are the 2027 HSA limits?

The IRS announced in Rev. Proc. 2026-24 that the 2027 limits are $4,500 for self-only coverage and $9,000 for family coverage. The catch-up amount stays at $1,000.

Can I have an HSA with a bronze plan?

Starting in 2026, bronze and catastrophic plans that are available as individual coverage through a marketplace Exchange are treated as HSA-eligible high deductible health plans, according to IRS Notice 2026-5. Small-employer SHOP bronze plans generally are not covered by that rule.

What happens if I spend HSA money on something that is not medical?

Before age 65 the amount is taxable income plus an additional 20% tax. After 65 there is no additional 20% tax, but the withdrawal is still taxed as ordinary income unless it pays for qualified medical expenses.

When is the deadline to contribute for 2026?

You can contribute for a tax year until the tax filing deadline, which is generally April 15 of the following year. For 2026 that means April 15, 2027. You report contributions on Form 8889.
