Standard Deduction or Itemize? How to Tell Which One Wins on Your Return

Most people pick one by default and never check. Here is the five-minute comparison that tells you which choice actually lowers your tax.

By Robert Allen Bautista, CPA candidate · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-19

Key takeaways

  • You subtract either the standard deduction or your itemized deductions, not both, and you generally pick the larger.
  • Itemizing happens on Schedule A (Form 1040). It only helps when your qualifying expenses add up to more than your standard deduction.
  • The standard deduction depends on filing status, age, blindness and whether someone can claim you as a dependent.
  • Business expenses on Schedule C are separate. They reduce your income no matter which choice you make.

Every filing season a common question is “Should I itemize?” Most people end up taking the standard deduction, and for many that is the right call. But it should be a choice you made on purpose, not a default you never looked at. The comparison takes about five minutes if you have your year-end paperwork nearby.

How the choice works

The IRS lets you reduce your income by either the standard deduction, a fixed amount set by law, or by itemized deductions, which are specific expenses you add up on Schedule A. You do not get both. In general you choose whichever is larger, because the larger number lowers your taxable income more. The standard deduction amount for the year is listed on irs.gov and in the Form 1040 instructions, and it changes with your filing status, whether you or your spouse are 65 or older or blind, and whether someone else can claim you as a dependent. I am not quoting a number here because it is adjusted and has been changed by recent legislation, so always check the current figure for your tax year on irs.gov.

What can go on Schedule A

The IRS instructions for Schedule A group itemized deductions into a few main categories:

  • Medical and dental expenses. Only the part that exceeds a percentage of your adjusted gross income counts. IRS Publication 502 gives the current percentage and the list of what qualifies.
  • Taxes you paid. This includes state and local income tax or sales tax, plus real estate and personal property tax. There is a limit on this category, and the limit has been revised by recent law, so confirm the current rule on irs.gov before you count on it.
  • Home mortgage interest. Your lender reports this on Form 1098. Limits apply based on the loan, so read the Schedule A instructions.
  • Gifts to charity. You need records. Cash gifts need a bank record or written acknowledgment, and larger or non-cash gifts have extra paperwork rules in IRS Publication 526.
  • Casualty and theft losses are limited to certain federally declared disaster situations.

The five-minute comparison

  1. Look up the standard deduction for your filing status on irs.gov for the tax year you are filing.
  2. Add up each Schedule A category using your actual documents: Form 1098, property tax bills, state tax paid, charitable receipts and medical costs.
  3. Compare the two totals. If your itemized total is not clearly larger, the standard deduction is usually simpler and no worse.
  4. If the totals are close, remember that itemizing also means you must keep records to support every line if the IRS asks.

Good tax software or a preparer will run both and show the result, but you will get better answers if you bring the documents. My tax season document checklist lists what to gather so that comparison is not guesswork.

When itemizing tends to make sense

Itemizing is more likely to win when you have several large costs in the same year. Typical situations include paying interest on a mortgage, paying substantial property and state taxes, making significant charitable gifts, or having medical costs that are high compared with your income. Someone who rents, has no mortgage and gives modestly will usually come out ahead with the standard deduction. A year with a large one-time cost, such as a major medical expense, can flip the answer, so do not assume last year’s choice still holds.

Married couples: the one rule that traps people

The IRS says that if you are married filing separately and your spouse itemizes, you cannot take the standard deduction and must itemize too, even if your own itemized total is small. If you and your spouse are weighing separate returns, compare both people’s numbers together before you decide, because this rule can change the result.

Self-employed: do not mix up the two kinds of deductions

If you work for yourself, the costs of running your business go on Schedule C and reduce your business profit. That happens whether you take the standard deduction or itemize. They are not Schedule A items. The home office is a common example, and the method is covered in my guide to the home office deduction for renters and homeowners. Treat business costs as their own track and compare standard against itemized only for your personal expenses.

Do not forget your state return

Missouri and Kansas each have their own rules for deductions, and they are not always identical to the federal choice. Check the current instructions and forms at dor.mo.gov and ksrevenue.gov before you assume the same approach carries over. If you live in one state and work in the other, you may file in both, so the details matter.

Records to keep if you itemize

  • Form 1098 and your year-end mortgage statement.
  • Property tax bills and proof of payment.
  • Charity receipts or written acknowledgments, dated and with the organization’s name.
  • Medical bills, insurance statements and mileage to appointments if you plan to claim it.

Keeping these in one folder from January onward makes the comparison fast and gives you proof if a question comes later.

A short example of the logic

Suppose a household adds up its mortgage interest, property and state taxes and charity and finds the total sits below its standard deduction. Itemizing would cost them time and record-keeping and lower their deduction, so the standard deduction wins. Now suppose a different household has a larger mortgage and a big medical year and the itemized total is clearly higher. For them Schedule A wins. The only way to know which household you are is to run the numbers with real documents.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take the standard deduction and itemize in the same year?

No. In general you choose one or the other on your federal return, and you pick the one that lowers your taxable income more.

Where do itemized deductions go?

On Schedule A, which you attach to Form 1040. The IRS instructions for Schedule A list the categories and limits.

Does my state follow my federal choice?

Not necessarily. Check the current forms and instructions at dor.mo.gov for Missouri and ksrevenue.gov for Kansas.
