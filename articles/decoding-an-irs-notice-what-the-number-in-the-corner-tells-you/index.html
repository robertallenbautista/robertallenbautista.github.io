Decoding an IRS Notice: What the Number in the Top-Right Corner Tells You

An envelope from the IRS is stressful. The good news is that every notice tells you what it is, what it wants and by when, if you know where to look.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • Look in the top-right corner for a notice or letter number such as CP2000, CP14 or CP501. That number tells you the type of notice.
  • Find the response date before you read anything else. Missing it can limit your options.
  • Many notices propose a change or ask for a payment. They are not a final decision, and you can often respond or disagree.
  • Never ignore a notice, and never act on a call, text or email that claims to be the IRS and demands immediate payment.
  • If you do not understand the notice, ask a tax professional to review it before you reply.

Most people open an IRS letter with a pounding heart and read the first paragraph three times. A calmer method works better. The IRS prints enough on each notice that you can identify it in about two minutes. I think of a notice as a form with a fixed layout, and the layout is the same whether the amount is $38 or $3,800.

Step one: read the corner, not the body

In the upper-right area of the first page, look for a code. It usually begins with CP (for notices) or the word Letter followed by numbers. Near it you will see the notice date, the tax year or period it covers, and often a partly masked taxpayer identification number. The code is the key. You can search that exact code on IRS.gov to find an explanation of the notice and what to do about it.

Step two: find the date and the dollar amount

Most notices state a response deadline in the body, commonly 30 days, though some give more or less. Circle it. Then find the amount, which is typically split into tax, penalties and interest. Check whether the notice covers the right year and whether the figures are plausible compared with your return. A notice is a claim, not proof of what you owe.

Common notices in general terms

CP2000: income does not match

This notice says the IRS received information from payers, such as 1099 or W-2 forms, that does not match what was on your return. It proposes a change and a new amount. It is not a bill yet. If the payer reported correctly, you may agree. If the form is wrong or you already included the income, you can disagree and send documentation. Sellers who received a 1099-K and reported only profit sometimes get this one, which I explain in my first 1099-K guide.

CP14: you owe a balance

This is often the first bill, sent when your return shows a balance that was not fully paid. It asks for payment by a date to avoid additional interest and penalties.

CP501, CP503 and CP504: reminders and escalation

If the balance remains unpaid, the IRS sends reminders. CP501 and CP503 are reminders, and CP504 is a stronger notice about collection steps such as taking a state refund. Later letters give you rights to request a hearing within a stated time. These deadlines are firm.

CP11 and CP12: the IRS changed your return

These notices say the IRS corrected a math error or other mistake on your return. CP12 usually means a refund changed. If you disagree, the notice explains the time you have to respond.

Identity verification letters

Some letters ask you to confirm your identity before processing a return. Use the contact instructions printed on the notice or go to the official IRS website, rather than a link in a message you received.

What to do in the first week

  1. Put the notice in a folder and write the response date on your calendar.
  2. Compare the notice with your filed return and any forms you received.
  3. Decide whether you agree, partly agree, or disagree.
  4. If you agree, pay or set up a payment through IRS.gov. If you cannot pay in full, ask about payment options rather than staying silent.
  5. If you disagree, respond in writing with copies, not originals, and keep proof of how and when you sent it.

What never to do

  • Never ignore it. Interest and penalties continue, and deadlines pass.
  • Never send original documents. Send copies.
  • Never pay a stranger who calls. The IRS generally first contacts people by mail. Be suspicious of anyone demanding gift cards, wire transfers or cryptocurrency.
  • Never guess at an answer. Respond to the exact question, with support.
  • Never assume the notice is correct, or that it is wrong. Verify it against records. If your records are scattered, that is a bookkeeping problem worth solving, and bookkeeping software or a bookkeeper is a good place to start.

When to get help

If a notice involves a large amount, multiple years, an examination request or a deadline that is close, have a tax professional review it before you respond. Frequently asked questions

Where is the notice number on an IRS letter?

Usually in the upper-right corner of the first page, as a code like CP2000 or CP14 or a Letter number. It is also sometimes printed at the bottom of the page.

Is a CP2000 a bill?

No. It proposes changes because information the IRS received does not match your return. You can agree, partly agree or disagree and respond by the date shown.

How long do I have to respond?

The deadline is printed on the notice, often around 30 days. Some notices allow more time or less. Always follow the date on your own letter.

What if I cannot pay what the notice says?

Do not ignore it. Look at the payment options on IRS.gov, such as an installment agreement, and contact the IRS before the deadline passes.

How do I know a notice is real?

A real notice arrives by mail with a code, date and your information. You can verify the code and instructions on IRS.gov. Do not respond to calls, texts or emails demanding payment.
