Paycheck Withholding Checkup: Is Your W-4 Set Up Right Before Year-End?

Your refund or balance due in April is mostly decided by your paycheck settings now. A ten-minute checkup in November or December can still change the outcome.

By Robert Allen Bautista, CPA candidate · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-18

Key takeaways

  • A big refund or a surprise balance due usually means your withholding was off all year.
  • The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator on irs.gov can show whether to adjust your Form W-4.
  • You can give your employer a new W-4 whenever your situation changes; a change applies to the pay periods that remain.
  • Missouri and Kansas have their own state withholding forms, MO W-4 and K-4.

Most people think of tax season as something that happens in the spring. In practice, a large share of your result is locked in by what your employer withholds from every paycheck. If the amount is too low, you owe in April. If it is too high, you gave the government an interest-free loan. Late in the year there is still time to fix it. Here is a checkup I walk clients through.

How withholding works

When you start a job, you fill out Form W-4, Employee’s Withholding Certificate. Your employer uses it to decide how much federal income tax to take out of each paycheck. At filing time, your actual tax is calculated on your return and compared with what was withheld. If more was withheld, you get a refund. If less, you pay the difference. The form has five steps: personal information, multiple jobs or a working spouse, claiming dependents, other adjustments, and your signature. The IRS says you only need to complete Steps 2 through 4 if they apply to you.

Who should do a checkup

The IRS recommends reviewing your withholding when your life changes. Common triggers include:

  • Marriage, divorce, or a change in a spouse’s job
  • A new baby or a child who no longer qualifies as a dependent
  • Starting a second job or a side business
  • Buying a home or a major change in deductions
  • A big raise, a bonus or unusual income, such as unemployment benefits or investment income

If you have a side hustle, withholding on your day job may not cover the tax on your freelance income. That is a common reason people owe more than they expected. My article on why your first freelance tax bill was so big covers that situation.

Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator

The IRS offers a free online tool called the Tax Withholding Estimator on irs.gov. You enter your filing status, income, withholding so far this year and similar details, and it suggests whether to change your W-4. Have your latest pay stub and last year’s return in front of you. The more current your inputs, the more useful the result. The IRS says the tool is designed to help you match your withholding to your tax liability, and it tells you what to change on a new Form W-4 if needed.

Your pay stub shows year-to-date federal withholding. Your W-2 in January shows the final total in Box 2, which is a good way to check your earlier estimate afterward.

Making a change

You do not file a new W-4 with the IRS. You give it to your employer, usually through payroll or HR. The IRS notes that you can submit a new Form W-4 whenever your situation changes. A change made now applies only to the pay periods still left in the year, so the per-paycheck adjustment needed to catch up is larger than it would have been in January. Ask your employer when the change will take effect.

If you owe because of side income

Sometimes the better fix is not a W-4 change at all. Self-employed income is not subject to withholding, and the IRS generally expects you to pay tax during the year through withholding, estimated tax payments or both. The IRS publishes safe harbor rules for avoiding an underpayment penalty, including paying a set percentage of the current year’s tax or of the prior year’s tax, with different rules for higher earners. Check the current rules on irs.gov for your income level. My estimated tax calendar for first-year Kansas City freelancers lays out the due dates.

Missouri and Kansas withholding

State withholding is separate from the federal W-4. Missouri has its own form, Form MO W-4, published by the Missouri Department of Revenue at dor.mo.gov. Kansas uses Form K-4, available from the Kansas Department of Revenue at ksrevenue.gov. If you live in one state and work in the other, ask your payroll department which state is being withheld, and check each department’s instructions. Kansas City also has an earnings tax, covered in my Kansas City earnings tax scenarios guide.

A quick checklist

  1. Pull your last pay stub and last year’s return.
  2. Run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator.
  3. If the result says to adjust, fill out a new Form W-4 and give it to your employer.
  4. Review your state form as well.
  5. In January, compare Box 2 on your W-2 to the estimate.

A worked example, with no numbers invented

Picture two coworkers at the same Kansas City employer earning the same wage. One claims a spouse and a child on the W-4 and has a spouse with income. The other is single, no dependents, and has a weekend business that earns extra money. Their paychecks can look similar, but their actual tax bills will not. The first may have too little withheld because the W-4 did not account for the spouse’s job. The second owes self-employment tax and income tax on the side income that nothing withheld. The estimator exists to sort out exactly this, because it asks about every income source and credit in one pass.

The lesson is that two paychecks that look alike can sit on very different tax situations. The only reliable test is to run your own numbers, and to rerun them after any change in the year.

Frequently asked questions

Do I send my W-4 to the IRS?

No. You give Form W-4 to your employer, who uses it to calculate withholding. You can submit a new one whenever your situation changes.

What is the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator?

It is a free tool on irs.gov that helps you compare your expected tax with your withholding and suggests whether to adjust your Form W-4.

Which state forms do Missouri and Kansas use for withholding?

Missouri uses Form MO W-4 and Kansas uses Form K-4. Check dor.mo.gov and ksrevenue.gov for current instructions.
