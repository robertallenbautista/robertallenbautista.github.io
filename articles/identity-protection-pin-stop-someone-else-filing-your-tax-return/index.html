IRS Identity Protection PIN: How to Stop Someone Else From Filing Your Return

The worst way to learn someone used your Social Security number is a rejected e-file in February. An IP PIN is one way to make that harder.

By Robert Allen Bautista, CPA candidate · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-18

Key takeaways

  • An Identity Protection PIN (IP PIN) is a six-digit number known only to you and the IRS.
  • It helps block someone else from filing a federal return with your Social Security number.
  • The IRS issues a new IP PIN each year; you need the current one when you file.
  • Share your IP PIN only with your trusted tax preparer, and never because of an email, text or call you did not start.

Tax identity theft usually shows up the same way. You file, and the IRS rejects your return because one using your Social Security number was already filed. By then a criminal may have claimed a refund that was yours. The IRS offers a free protection called an Identity Protection PIN that many people never hear about until it is too late. Here is how it works.

What an IP PIN is

The IRS describes an Identity Protection PIN as a six-digit number assigned to eligible taxpayers to help prevent misuse of their Social Security number or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number on fraudulent federal income tax returns. It works like a second password. When you e-file, you enter the PIN. If it is missing or wrong, the IRS will not accept the return. That means a thief with your name and Social Security number alone cannot file in your name.

Who can get one

The IRS says identity theft victims are enrolled in the program after the IRS resolves their case. The IRS also lets other taxpayers who can verify their identity opt in voluntarily, even if they have never been a victim. The program covers the primary taxpayer, a spouse and dependents, and each person has their own PIN.

How to get one

The fastest route, according to the IRS, is the Get an IP PIN tool, which you reach through an IRS online account at irs.gov. You go through identity verification first. If you cannot verify online, the IRS describes alternatives, including Form 15227 if you meet its requirements or an in-person appointment at a Taxpayer Assistance Center with identification. Check irs.gov for the current eligibility rules before choosing a route, because they have changed over time.

How it works year to year

  • The IRS issues a new IP PIN each calendar year, so last year’s number will not work.
  • You enter it on your return when you file. Tax software has a field for it.
  • If you use a preparer, you give them the number so they can include it. Your preparer needs the PIN for every person on the return who has one, including a spouse and any dependents.
  • If you lose it, retrieve it through your IRS online account. Do not guess.

Never hand it out casually

The IRS warns that it does not initiate contact about your IP PIN by email, text or social media. A message saying your PIN expired or needs verification is a scam. Share the number only with your tax preparer, and only when preparing your return. If you are shopping for a preparer, this article on ghost preparers and PTIN checks covers the red flags worth knowing.

If your return is rejected

A reject for a duplicate Social Security number can mean a typo, a dependent claimed on someone else’s return, or identity theft. Do not assume the worst, but do not ignore it either.

  1. Check your numbers and spellings against the Social Security cards.
  2. If you have a dependent, confirm whether someone else could be claiming them.
  3. If you believe your identity was misused, the IRS directs you to Form 14039, Identity Theft Affidavit.
  4. Report it at identitytheft.gov, the Federal Trade Commission site, and follow the recovery steps there.
  5. Respond to any IRS verification letter using the instructions on the letter and on irs.gov, not a link in a text.

If a letter arrives and you are unsure what it is, decoding an IRS notice helps you identify it by the number in the corner and decide what to do next.

Do not forget the state side

An IP PIN covers federal returns. Missouri and Kansas returns can also be targeted. If you suspect identity theft, check the Missouri Department of Revenue at dor.mo.gov and the Kansas Department of Revenue at ksrevenue.gov for how to report it and any state-specific steps.

Other habits that help

  • File as soon as you have every form, since a thief needs to file before you do.
  • Use a strong, unique password and multi-factor authentication on your IRS online account.
  • Shred paperwork containing your Social Security number.
  • Question any income document that shows wages or payments you never received.

What a thief usually wants

The motive is almost always a refund. A criminal files early with stolen information and false wages, directs the refund somewhere they control, and counts on the real taxpayer filing later. That is why the early part of filing season matters. It is also why a return that is complete and filed promptly, with an IP PIN, makes you a harder target than one left open until April.

What this means for dependents and families

Parents sometimes assume identity protection is only for adults. The IRS says dependents can have an IP PIN too, so a child’s Social Security number can be protected from misuse. If you are the parent or guardian, keep the numbers with the family tax folder, and bring all of them to your preparer. A missing PIN for one person on the return is enough to cause a reject. Frequently asked questions

What is an IRS Identity Protection PIN?

It is a six-digit number known only to you and the IRS that helps prevent someone else from filing a federal return using your Social Security number or ITIN.

Does the IP PIN change every year?

Yes. The IRS issues a new IP PIN each calendar year, and you need the current number when you file.

Who should I give my IP PIN to?

Only your trusted tax preparer, when preparing your return. The IRS does not ask for it by email, text or social media.
