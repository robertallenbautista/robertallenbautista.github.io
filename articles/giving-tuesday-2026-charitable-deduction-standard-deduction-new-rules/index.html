Giving Tuesday 2026: The New Charitable Deduction If You Take the Standard Deduction

Giving Tuesday is December 1. This year a cash gift can lower your tax bill even if you never itemize, and a few other rules changed too.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator · Published 2026-11-21

Key takeaways

  • Starting with tax year 2026, taxpayers who take the standard deduction can deduct up to $1,000 of cash gifts to qualified charities, or $2,000 on a joint return.
  • If you itemize, 2026 gifts are deductible only to the extent they exceed 0.5% of your AGI.
  • Itemized deductions, including charity, are reduced by 2/37 for people whose income reaches the 37% bracket.
  • Gifts of $250 or more need a written acknowledgment from the charity. Noncash gifts over $500 go on Form 8283.
  • IRA owners age 70½ or older can send up to $111,000 directly to charity in 2026 without counting it as income.

Giving Tuesday falls on December 1, 2026, and the tax year closes a month later. That timing matters more than usual, because the charitable rules changed for 2026. The changes come from Public Law 119-21, the 2025 law commonly called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which the IRS now calls Working Families Tax Cuts. Some changes help people who give a little. Others make it harder to deduct the first slice of a larger gift.

The new deduction if you take the standard deduction

Most taxpayers do not itemize, so for them a donation used to change nothing on the return. Section 70424 of the law raised and made permanent a deduction for people who skip itemizing. According to the IRS reminder of November 10, 2026, you may deduct up to $1,000 of qualifying cash contributions, or $2,000 for married couples filing jointly, beginning with tax year 2026.

The rules to keep straight:

  • Cash only. Noncash gifts such as clothing or stock do not qualify.
  • Not every charity counts. Cash given to a supporting organization, or to set up or fund a donor advised fund, does not qualify.
  • Made during the tax year. Prior-year carryovers do not qualify.
  • Only if you do not itemize. It is an alternative to Schedule A, not an add-on.

The standard deduction for 2026 is $16,100 for single filers and $32,200 for married couples filing jointly, per the IRS inflation adjustment release. Unless your itemized deductions would beat those numbers, the non-itemizer deduction is probably the one you will use.

A quick example

These numbers are made up to show the math. A single filer takes the standard deduction and gives $1,400 in cash to qualified charities during 2026. Only $1,000 is deductible, so $400 produces no federal tax benefit. A married couple filing jointly gives $2,600 and can deduct $2,000. Now take someone who itemizes with AGI of $120,000 and cash gifts of $5,000. The floor is 0.5% × $120,000 = $600, so the deduction is $5,000 − $600 = $4,400.

The 0.5% floor if you itemize

Section 70425 of the law added a floor for people who itemize. Under the new text of section 170(b)(1)(I), a charitable contribution is allowed only to the extent the total exceeds 0.5% of your contribution base for the year. For individuals that base is generally AGI. The floor applies to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025.

The same section rewrote the cash limit so that gifts to public charities can be deducted up to 60% of that base for taxable years after 2017, which keeps the higher cash limit in place going forward. In practice, the floor mostly trims modest gifts. If your AGI is $80,000, the first $400 of gifts does nothing for an itemizer. The law also adds carryover rules for amounts held back by the other percentage limits, and they are technical enough that a preparer should run them.

What changed for high earners

There is no special cap on charitable gifts for the wealthy. The change sits in section 70111, which rewrote section 68. For taxable years after 2025, itemized deductions are reduced by 2/37 of the lesser of your itemized deductions or the part of your taxable income above where the 37% bracket begins. It applies to all itemized deductions together, charity included, and it only matters if your income reaches that bracket.

Here is the arithmetic. If a $10,000 deduction is cut by 2/37, you are left with $9,459.46. At 37%, that saves $3,500, which is 35% of the original $10,000. For a taxpayer who is fully subject to the reduction, each charitable dollar can save about 35 cents of federal income tax instead of 37.

Records that protect the deduction

The IRS says in Tax Tip 2026-57 that you should keep proof of every cash donation. A bank record or a written communication from the charity showing its name, the date and the amount works. Publication 526 lists canceled checks, bank or credit card statements and electronic transfer receipts.

  • $250 or more. You need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity. It must give the cash amount or describe the property and say whether you received goods or services in return. You must have it by the earlier of the date you file or the due date, including extensions.
  • Noncash over $500. If your deduction for a noncash gift is more than $500, file Form 8283 with your return. Items over $5,000 generally need a qualified appraisal and Section B, though publicly traded securities are reported in Section A.
  • Timing. A gift charged to a credit card is deductible in the year you make the charge. A check counts on the date you mail it. A December 31 charge or mailing date counts for 2026.

Noncash gifts such as clothing and household goods do not qualify for the standard-deduction filer's new deduction, but they can still be deducted if you itemize, and the same recordkeeping applies.

Giving from an IRA after age 70½

A qualified charitable distribution, or QCD, is a nontaxable distribution made directly by the trustee of your IRA to an organization eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions. Per Publication 590-B, you must be at least 70½ when it is made, and the IRA cannot be an ongoing SEP or SIMPLE. You also need the same acknowledgment you would need for a deduction.

For 2026 the limit is $111,000 per person, up from $108,000, according to IRS Notice 2025-67. If you file jointly, your spouse can also make a QCD from a separate IRA. A QCD counts toward your required minimum distribution, and you cannot also claim it as a charitable deduction. That second point is why a QCD helps people who take the standard deduction: the money leaves the IRA without raising your income, and no itemizing is needed. The notice also raises the one-time election to fund a charitable gift annuity or remainder trust from $54,000 to $55,000.

Donating appreciated stock

Stock you have held for more than one year is capital gain property under Publication 526. If you itemize, the deduction for it is generally its fair market value on the day of the gift, and you do not reduce it by the appreciation. The limit for such gifts to 50% limit organizations is generally 30% of your AGI, compared with 60% for cash. Stock held one year or less is treated differently, and the deduction is generally limited to your basis.

Gifts of stock do not count toward the non-itemizer deduction, so this move only helps if you itemize. Report it on Form 8283, Section A, if the deduction is over $500, and keep the charity's acknowledgment showing the issuer, the type of security and whether it is publicly traded. Transfers take time, so start well before the last week of December.

Frequently asked questions

Can I deduct charitable gifts if I take the standard deduction in 2026?

Yes, up to a limit. Beginning with tax year 2026, people who do not itemize can deduct up to $1,000 of qualifying cash gifts, or $2,000 on a joint return. Noncash gifts, gifts to donor advised funds and gifts to supporting organizations do not count.

What is the 0.5% floor for charitable gifts?

If you itemize, 2026 charitable contributions are deductible only to the extent they exceed 0.5% of your contribution base, which is generally your adjusted gross income. With $120,000 of AGI, the first $600 of gifts would not be deductible.

What records do I need for a donation?

For any cash gift you need a bank record or a written record from the charity showing its name, the date and the amount. For a gift of $250 or more you need a written acknowledgment from the charity. For noncash gifts over $500 you file Form 8283.

What is a qualified charitable distribution from an IRA?

It is a nontaxable transfer made directly by your IRA trustee to a charity, available once you are 70½ or older. The IRS set the 2026 annual limit at $111,000 per person. You cannot also deduct the amount as a charitable gift.

Can I donate appreciated stock?

Yes, if you itemize. For stock held more than one year, the deduction is generally the fair market value on the date of the gift, subject to a 30% of AGI limit for gifts to 50% limit organizations. Stock gifts do not qualify for the new non-itemizer deduction.
