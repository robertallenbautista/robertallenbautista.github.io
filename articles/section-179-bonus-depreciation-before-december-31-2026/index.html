Section 179 and Bonus Depreciation Before December 31: Buying Equipment or a Work Vehicle in Kansas City

A year-end purchase can lower your tax, but only if the business needed the equipment anyway.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator, CPA candidate · Published 2026-11-25

Key takeaways

  • According to IRS Publication 946, the maximum Section 179 deduction for tax years beginning in 2026 is $2,560,000, and it starts to shrink above $4,090,000 of Section 179 property placed in service.
  • The maximum Section 179 deduction for sport utility vehicles is $32,000, which can matter more than the headline number for a small business.
  • Property has to be placed in service, ready and available for its intended use in the business, by the end of the year. A purchase order or a deposit is not enough.
  • A deduction lowers your taxable income and does not return the money you spent, so buy for the business and not for the deadline.

Every December, business owners hear the same advice: buy something before the year ends and write it off. Sometimes that advice is sound. Other times you spend real money to save a smaller amount of tax. This article explains the two tools behind the advice, the 2026 numbers, and how to decide whether a purchase makes sense.

December 31, 2026 falls on a Thursday, so there is little time left. Bring this article to your preparer as a checklist.

Two ways to deduct equipment in year one

Normally, when a business buys equipment, the cost is depreciated over several years. Two rules let you take much more of the cost in the first year.

Section 179 lets you elect to deduct the cost of qualifying property in the year you put it in service, up to a limit. It also has a phase-down for businesses that buy a lot of equipment in a year.

Bonus depreciation, which the IRS calls the special depreciation allowance, is an extra first-year deduction on qualifying property. Each tool has its own limits and conditions, so ask your preparer how they apply to your business income.

The 2026 numbers

According to IRS Publication 946, for tax years beginning in 2026:

  • The maximum Section 179 deduction is $2,560,000.
  • The deduction starts to shrink dollar for dollar once the cost of Section 179 property placed in service during the year goes above $4,090,000.
  • The maximum Section 179 deduction for sport utility vehicles is $32,000.

Most small businesses in the Kansas City area will never come near the first two figures. The SUV cap catches people off guard. If you are thinking about a heavy vehicle for the business, the SUV limit may matter more than the headline number.

For bonus depreciation, Publication 946 explains that the 2025 law (Public Law 119-21) reinstated a 100% allowance for qualifying property acquired and placed in service after January 19, 2025. The 2025 section of the publication also describes an option for the first tax year ending after that date to choose a lower allowance of 40% instead. Check with your preparer about how this applies to a purchase in 2026, since the 2026 section of the publication does not repeat the rule.

What "placed in service" means

This phrase decides which year you get the deduction. A purchase order or a deposit is not enough. The property needs to be ready and available for its intended use in the business by the end of the year. A truck that is ordered in December and delivered in January belongs to next year's return. Keep the delivery receipt, the invoice and any record of the date you started using the item.

The business also has to use the property for the business. A vehicle used part of the time for personal driving gets a smaller deduction, and the records you keep decide how much. A mileage log or a calendar is the usual proof.

Spending a dollar to save a few cents

A deduction lowers your taxable income. It does not return the money you spent. If you spend $10,000 on equipment and deduct all $10,000, your tax savings equal your tax rate times $10,000, which is a fraction of the $10,000 you spent.

Here is a made-up example. A sole proprietor in the Kansas City area has no plan to buy anything, then buys a $10,000 piece of equipment in late December just for the deduction. If her combined tax rate on the next $10,000 of profit is around 30%, the tax savings are about $3,000. She is out $7,000 of cash that she might have kept. The purchase pays off only if the business needed the equipment anyway.

The reverse also happens. A business that planned to buy equipment in January can often get the same deduction a few weeks sooner by buying in December. The cash outlay is the same, and the deduction arrives a year earlier.

Questions to ask yourself first

Before you buy for tax reasons, think through these.

  • Would the business buy this equipment anyway within the next year?
  • Will next year's income be much higher than this year's? A big deduction may be worth more in a higher-income year, and you can sometimes choose to depreciate the cost over time instead.
  • Are you financing the purchase? The deduction may be available even when you borrow, but you still owe the payments.
  • Will you sell the equipment soon? Ask what a sale would do to the deduction you already took.
  • How does the deduction affect other items on your return, like the qualified business income deduction or your health insurance credit?

State rules can also differ from federal rules. Ask your preparer how Missouri or Kansas treats these deductions, since a state may handle depreciation differently.

Records to keep

For each purchase you deduct this way, keep:

  • The invoice or bill of sale showing the date and price.
  • Proof of payment.
  • The date the item was first used in the business.
  • A description of how it is used and, for a vehicle, a mileage log.
  • Any financing paperwork.

You will show these papers if the IRS asks about the deduction later.

Making the election on your return

A Section 179 deduction is an election. You make it on Form 4562 with your return. Bonus depreciation has its own rules about when it applies and when you can choose a different treatment, so tell your preparer what you bought and what result you want before the return is filed.

A note on timing

If December 31 is days away, do not buy something just to reach the deadline. Rushed purchases can come with higher prices or poor warranties. A smaller deduction on equipment the business needs beats a larger deduction on equipment it does not.

Questions to ask your preparer

  • Does my business income support a full deduction this year?
  • Is it better to take the deduction now or spread it over several years?
  • If I buy a vehicle, what limits apply to it, and what mileage records do I need?
  • How do Missouri or Kansas treat this deduction compared with the federal rule?
  • What happens to the deduction if I sell the equipment in a few years?

Frequently asked questions

What is the Section 179 limit for 2026?

According to IRS Publication 946, the maximum Section 179 deduction for tax years beginning in 2026 is $2,560,000. The deduction starts to shrink dollar for dollar once the cost of Section 179 property placed in service during the year goes above $4,090,000.

Does a truck ordered in December count for this year?

Only if it is placed in service by December 31. A truck that is ordered in December and delivered in January belongs to next year's return. Keep the delivery receipt, the invoice and a record of the date you started using it.

Is there a separate limit for SUVs?

Yes. IRS Publication 946 lists a $32,000 maximum Section 179 deduction for sport utility vehicles for tax years beginning in 2026.
