CP2000 Notice Explained: What the IRS Is Actually Telling You

A CP2000 looks like a bill, but it is not one. Here is what the notice means, why it showed up, and what the IRS wants from you.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-19

Key takeaways

  • A CP2000 says income or payment information the IRS received from others does not match your return. It proposes changes and is not a bill.
  • The IRS asks for a reply within 30 days of the notice date, or 60 days if you live outside the United States.
  • You can agree, partly agree or disagree on the response form, and you can send documents to back up your side.
  • Interest generally runs from the original due date of the return, and it keeps growing until the balance is paid.
  • If the IRS hears nothing by the response date, it sends a Statutory Notice of Deficiency.

A CP2000 can look like a bill, but the IRS says it is not one. The page is full of numbers, there is a dollar amount at the bottom, and the letter says the IRS thinks you underreported. Take a breath. The notice is easier to handle once you know what it is and what it is not.

What a CP2000 actually is

The IRS describes the CP2000 on its page Understanding your CP2000 notice. It says the notice means income or payment information the IRS received from third parties, such as employers or financial institutions, does not match the information on your return. It proposes changes to your tax, and the IRS states plainly that it is not a bill and that a response may be required.

It is sometimes called an underreporter notice. The IRS explains in Topic no. 652 that an automated system, the Automated Underreporter program, compares what third parties reported to what you reported, and then a tax examiner reviews any possible mismatch. A computer flagged it and a person looked at it, but nobody has decided you did anything wrong. The proposed change is a starting point.

Why a mismatch happens

The IRS page points to a few situations to check:

  • Someone used your name and Social Security number. The IRS points to Form 14039 for that.
  • Income information the IRS received after you filed. The IRS says to report that on an amended return.
  • The same issue in earlier years. The IRS says to check your prior returns.
  • Third-party income or payment information that simply does not match what you reported.

How to read the notice

Start with the response date near the top. The IRS asks you to respond within 30 days of the date of the notice, or 60 days if you live outside the United States, per Topic no. 652. Then look for three things:

  1. The items in question. The notice lists the income, deduction or credit information it could not match.
  2. The proposed change. This shows how the IRS would change your tax if you agreed.
  3. The response form. If the notice includes one, it is where you tell the IRS whether you agree or disagree.

Topic no. 652 also notes that the CP2000 generally includes interest calculated from the due date of the return, and that certain penalties may also apply but may not be shown on the notice. So the number on the page may not be the final number.

Your three response options

According to the IRS page, if the notice includes a response form, you complete and sign it, mark whether you agree or disagree, and attach supporting documents.

  • You agree. Sign and return the form, then pay the amount or wait for a bill. If you agree and have nothing to add, the IRS says you do not need to amend your return.
  • You agree, but you also have other income, credits or expenses. The IRS says to file Form 1040-X, write “CP2000” at the top, and send it with your response. My article on Form 1040-X walks through that form.
  • You disagree with some or all of it. Mark the box on the response form, write a signed explanation and attach documents that support your position.

How to send your response

The IRS says the fastest option is to upload your response and documents using its online upload tool, with the access code printed on your notice. You can also fax to the number for the IRS location in the top left of your notice, or mail it to the address listed there. The IRS asks you to check the privacy and security policies of any online fax service you use. Keep a copy of everything and write down the date you sent it.

If you need more time, the IRS page says you can request an extension by sending your request through one of those reply options. Do not just let the date pass.

What happens if you do nothing

Per Topic no. 652, if the IRS does not hear from you by the response date, it will send a Statutory Notice of Deficiency. The IRS explains on its appeal opportunities page that a notice of deficiency gives you 90 days from the notice date to dispute the proposed changes without paying first, per the IRS page. Interest keeps accruing until any balance is paid, so silence rarely helps.

When to bring in help

You can authorize someone to speak with the IRS for you, either through the authorization section on the response form or with Form 2848 for a representative. The IRS page also points to the Taxpayer Advocate Service and Low Income Taxpayer Clinics for free help, and to Publication 5181 for more detail on how these reviews work. If the notice involves someone using your identity, the IRS says to send Form 14039 with your reply. If the notice makes you unsure, my own guide to decoding IRS notices is a good companion.

If you cannot pay in full, the IRS page mentions payment plans and offers in compromise. Ask about those before you ignore a balance.

Frequently asked questions

Is a CP2000 a bill?

No. The IRS says the notice proposes changes and is not a bill, and that a response may be required. A bill may follow if the matter is not resolved.

How long do I have to respond to a CP2000?

The IRS asks you to respond within 30 days of the date of the notice, or 60 days if you live outside the United States. Check the date printed on your own notice.

What if I disagree with the proposed changes?

Mark the disagree box on the response form, add a signed explanation and attach documents that support your position, then send it back by upload, fax or mail.
