Form 1099-DA and Crypto on Your 2026 Return: What Brokers Report and What You Track

The new crypto form helps, but it will not do your whole return for you.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-24

Key takeaways

  • Form 1099-DA reports digital asset proceeds from broker transactions. Brokers report on it for transactions on or after January 1, 2025, so your 2026 return is the second year.
  • For 2025 sales, brokers reported gross proceeds. Starting in 2026, brokers must also report basis for covered digital assets acquired after 2025 in a custodial account.
  • If you bought before 2026 or moved crypto between places, the form may show proceeds with no basis, and the burden to prove basis is yours.
  • You still answer the digital asset question on Form 1040 and report sales on Form 8949, and you keep your own records.

Crypto holders now get a tax form that looks a lot like the one stock investors have had for years. The form is Form 1099-DA, Digital Asset Proceeds From Broker Transactions. If you sold, swapped or spent digital assets through an exchange or other broker, expect one when you file your 2026 return in early 2027.

The new form helps, but it will not do your whole return for you. Some of what it reports is partial, and the rest of the work stays with you.

What the form is

The IRS describes Form 1099-DA as the form used to report digital asset proceeds from broker transactions. Brokers are required to report on this form beginning with transactions on or after January 1, 2025. So the 2025 return, filed in early 2026, was the first year of the form. Your 2026 return is the second.

Gross proceeds first, basis in stages

Basis is what you paid for an asset, plus certain costs. You subtract basis from proceeds to find your gain or loss. The 1099-DA is still catching up on this point.

For sales in 2025, brokers reported gross proceeds. Starting in 2026, brokers must also report basis for what the rules call covered securities. According to the IRS instructions for the 2026 form, covered digital assets are those acquired after 2025 in a custodial account that the broker holds until the sale. If you bought crypto at an exchange in 2026 and sold it there in 2026, there is a good chance the form shows both proceeds and basis.

If you bought your crypto before 2026, or moved it from one place to another, the broker may not know your cost. Basis reporting for those noncovered assets is voluntary. A broker that reports it anyway checks a box on the form. If the box is not checked, the number may be incomplete, and the burden to prove your basis is yours.

The IRS also says brokers cannot use acquisition information you provide to them to report basis. A broker may use it only to pick which lot you are selling. So the form may show proceeds with no basis even when you gave the broker your purchase records.

What you still track yourself

The IRS says that if you have digital asset transactions, you must report them whether or not they result in a taxable gain or loss. In practice, that means a few things.

You still answer the digital asset question on Form 1040. The question asks whether, at any time during the year, you received a digital asset as a reward, award or payment, or sold, exchanged or otherwise disposed of one. The IRS says to answer yes if you had those transactions. You answer no if you only held assets, or moved them between wallets you control without paying a fee in digital assets.

You still report sales and other dispositions of digital assets held as a capital asset on Form 8949. Other digital asset income, such as income from forks, staking or mining, goes on Schedule 1 of Form 1040.

You also keep your own records of purchases, receipts, sales, exchanges and other dispositions. The IRS points out that the Code and regulations require sufficient records to support what you put on your return. A 1099-DA is one record among several you should keep.

The basis gap problem

Compare the form to your own records before you file. Several common problems cause a mismatch.

  • You moved crypto from one exchange to another. The new broker may see a deposit with no cost attached.
  • You bought on one exchange and sold on another.
  • You paid a transaction fee that does not show up in the broker's numbers.
  • The form lumps together sales that you track separately.
  • You used a decentralized exchange or a wallet that is not a broker and received no form at all.

If the proceeds on your 1099-DA are right but the basis is blank or wrong, you still report the sale. You use the correct basis from your records and, where the form requires it, you adjust it on Form 8949.

The IRS also describes Revenue Procedure 2024-28, which lets taxpayers allocate unused basis to the remaining units in their wallets or accounts as of January 1, 2025. If you held crypto across that date, ask your preparer whether you made that allocation and where it is documented.

A made-up example

This is a made-up example with round numbers. Say you buy some coins on one exchange in 2024 and move them to another exchange in 2025. In 2026 you sell them there for $5,000. The second exchange sends a 1099-DA showing $5,000 of proceeds and no basis, because the coins were not bought there. Your records show you paid $3,000. You report the $5,000 and your $3,000 basis, and the taxable gain is $2,000, before considering how long you held them. If you only entered the number on the form with no basis, you would be reporting $5,000 as gain, which overstates your income by $3,000.

Records to gather in January

Collect these before you meet with a preparer.

  • Every 1099-DA you receive, including any corrected versions.
  • Exchange transaction histories for the whole year, downloaded as a file you can save.
  • Purchase records for assets you sold, including the date and cost.
  • Records of any crypto you received as payment, with the dollar value on the day you got it.
  • A list of the wallets and accounts you use.

Download your history early. Some exchanges limit how far back their reports go, and some close accounts.

What the IRS is likely to compare

With a 1099-DA, the IRS receives the same proceeds figures that you do. An unreported sale is easier to spot than it used to be, so enter every form you get, even if the number looks small.

  • How should I handle crypto I moved between exchanges?
  • Did I answer the digital asset question on Form 1040 correctly?
  • Do I need to document basis from before 2025?
  • How should I report income from staking, mining or crypto I got as payment?

Frequently asked questions

What is Form 1099-DA?

The IRS describes Form 1099-DA as the form used to report digital asset proceeds from broker transactions. Brokers are required to report on it beginning with transactions on or after January 1, 2025.

Will my 1099-DA show my cost basis?

Sometimes. Starting in 2026, brokers must report basis for covered digital assets, which the IRS instructions describe as those acquired after 2025 in a custodial account the broker holds until the sale. If you bought earlier or moved crypto from another place, the broker may not know your cost, and the form may show proceeds with no basis.

Do I still report crypto if I do not get a 1099-DA?

Yes. The IRS says that if you have digital asset transactions, you must report them whether or not they result in a taxable gain or loss. You also keep your own records of purchases, receipts, sales, exchanges and other dispositions.
