Home Office Deduction for Renters and Owners: Which Method, and What to Keep

Whether you rent or own changes how a home office deduction works. Here is how to choose a method and keep the proof that holds up.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator, CPA candidate · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • The deduction requires space used regularly and exclusively for your business, and it must be your principal place of business or a place you meet clients.
  • The simplified method gives $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet, and needs almost no paperwork.
  • The regular method uses actual costs times the business percentage of your home. Renters use rent, homeowners use mortgage interest, taxes and more.
  • W-2 employees generally cannot claim a home office deduction, even if they work from home full time.
  • Measurements, photos and utility bills are the records that matter most. Check IRS.gov for current rules.

The home office deduction is one of the most common questions self-employed people have. They want to know if it is risky, whether it applies to renters and which method to pick. The honest answer is that it is one of the more valuable deductions for a self-employed person, and one of the easier ones to support, if you keep the right records.

Who qualifies

Two tests apply to the space. It must be used regularly and exclusively for your business. A desk in a bedroom works, if the desk area is only for work. A kitchen table you also eat at does not. And it must be your principal place of business (including where you do administrative work and have no other fixed location) or a place where you meet clients in the normal course of business.

One more point is easy to miss. If you are a W-2 employee, you generally cannot deduct a home office, even if your employer requires you to work remotely. That deduction is only for the self-employed and for certain other narrow categories. Check IRS.gov for the current rules. If you have both a W-2 job and a freelance business, the space must be used for the business.

The two methods

Simplified method

You multiply the square feet of the office, up to 300, by a fixed rate of $5. The maximum is $1,500. You do not track rent, utilities or depreciation. You still need the measurement and proof the space qualifies. There is no carryover of unused amounts.

Regular method

You find the business-use percentage by dividing office square footage by the total square footage of the home. Then you apply that percentage to eligible costs and report the result on Form 8829. This often produces a larger deduction for people with higher housing costs, but it needs more records.

Renters

If you rent, the regular method uses a share of your rent, renters insurance and utilities. A 120-square-foot office in a 1,000-square-foot apartment is 12%. With $1,400 in monthly rent, that is $168 a month before utilities, or about $2,016 a year. You have no depreciation and no mortgage interest. The simplified method would give $600 for the same room ($5 times 120), so the comparison is worth doing. Renters with moderate rent often find the regular method comes out higher, but the gap shrinks with a small office.

Homeowners

Homeowners can include mortgage interest, real estate taxes, homeowners insurance, utilities, repairs that benefit the office and depreciation on the business portion of the house. Depreciation increases the deduction but can affect your tax when you sell, so it is worth a conversation before choosing the regular method. With the simplified method, your mortgage interest and property taxes remain available as itemized deductions if you itemize, while with the regular method the business share moves to the business form. Either way, you do not lose the cost, but where it appears changes.

How to choose

  1. Compute the simplified amount: square feet times $5, capped at $1,500.
  2. Compute the regular amount using your actual annual costs and business percentage.
  3. Compare the difference against the time cost of the records.
  4. Remember the limit: the deduction cannot exceed the income from the business use of the home, with unused regular-method amounts carrying forward.
  5. You can switch methods from year to year.

What records to keep

  • A drawing or a measurement of the office and of the whole home.
  • Dated photos showing the space is set up for work.
  • Lease or mortgage statements, and property tax and insurance bills.
  • Twelve months of utility bills.
  • Notes on how the room is used, and a simple log if you see clients there.

Store them where your bookkeeping lives, as I describe in bookkeeping software or a bookkeeper. The preparer's side of this is covered in what a preparer checks before signing your return.

Common mistakes

  • Claiming a room that is also a guest room or a playroom.
  • Using an estimate of square footage instead of a measurement.
  • Forgetting that the deduction is limited by business income.
  • Claiming it as an employee. Even a home office for a remote W-2 job generally does not qualify.

The deduction lowers income tax, and it also lowers self-employment tax, since both are based on net profit, which ties back to why your first freelance tax bill was so big. If you want help comparing methods, read the about me page for how I work.

Frequently asked questions

Can renters claim the home office deduction?

Yes, if the space meets the regular and exclusive use tests and the business is yours. Renters deduct a share of rent, renters insurance and utilities under the regular method, or use the simplified method.

Can I claim a home office if I work remotely for an employer?

W-2 employees generally cannot, even if working from home is required. The deduction is mainly for self-employed people. Check IRS.gov for current rules.

Does the home office deduction trigger an audit?

Claiming it does not automatically cause one. A correct claim with measurements, photos and bills is supportable. Problems arise when the space does not meet the exclusive use test.

What is the maximum under the simplified method?

$5 per square foot up to 300 square feet, for a maximum of $1,500. Confirm the current rate on IRS.gov.

Do I have to use the same method every year?

No. You can choose the simplified or regular method each year, but you cannot carry over unused simplified amounts.
