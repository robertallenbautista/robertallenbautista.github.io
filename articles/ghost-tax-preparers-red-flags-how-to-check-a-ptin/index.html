Ghost Tax Preparers: Red Flags and How to Check Who Is Preparing Your Return

Anyone can hang a sign that says tax service in February. Before you hand over your Social Security number, here is how to tell a real preparer from a risky one.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-16

Key takeaways

  • Anyone paid to prepare a federal return generally must have a PTIN and must sign the return and list that PTIN on it.
  • A ghost preparer prepares your return, then refuses to sign it, which leaves you holding the risk.
  • Warning signs include fees based on the size of your refund, promises of a bigger refund, and asking you to sign a blank return.
  • Ask for the preparer’s PTIN and credentials, and use the IRS directory to look up preparers with credentials such as CPAs, attorneys and enrolled agents.
  • Make sure your refund goes to an account in your own name.

Tax season brings pop-up storefronts, social media ads and a few preparers who vanish by May. Most paid preparers are honest and competent, but the ones who are not can cost you a lot: wrong refunds, penalties, even identity theft. Here is what to look for, and how to check a preparer before you sign anything. The IRS calls the worst offenders “ghost preparers,” and its guidance on them is a good starting point.

What is a ghost preparer?

A ghost preparer does the work of preparing your return but will not sign it. The IRS requires anyone who is paid to prepare a federal return to sign it and include a Preparer Tax Identification Number, or PTIN, in the paid preparer section. A preparer who skips this is a red flag. The IRS says a refusal to sign can mean the preparer is looking to make a quick profit, for example by promising a big refund or charging a fee based on the size of your refund. Either way, the return is still yours. You are the one responsible for what is on it.

Red flags to watch for

  • They will not sign. Some will tell you to mail in the printed return yourself, or “self-prepare” by signing alone. Walk away.
  • Fees based on your refund. A percentage of the refund is a classic warning sign, and the IRS tells taxpayers to avoid preparers who charge a percentage of the refund. Honest fees are tied to the work, not the outcome.
  • Guaranteed or inflated refunds. No one can guarantee a refund before seeing your documents. The IRS warns about preparers who promise large refunds, exaggerate eligibility for deductions or claim credits you do not qualify for.
  • Blank or incomplete forms. Never sign a return with empty lines.
  • Refund routed elsewhere. Your refund should go directly to a bank account in your name. Be wary if the preparer wants any part of it deposited in their own account.
  • Cash only, no receipt. The IRS lists cash-only payment with no receipt as a ghost preparer sign. A legitimate business gives you a receipt and a written fee.
  • Asking you to skip your documents. A preparer who says you do not need to bring a W-2 or 1099 and will “just estimate” is guessing with your return.
  • No copy for you. You should receive a full copy of your return, and the IRS advises making sure your preparer offers IRS e-file.

Check the PTIN

A PTIN is issued by the IRS to paid preparers and starts with the letter P followed by eight digits. Ask for it. A real preparer will share it, because it already appears on every return they sign. Seeing it on a business card or the return is not proof that the number belongs to them, so pair the number with other checks.

Look up credentials

The IRS maintains a public Directory of Federal Tax Return Preparers with Credentials and Select Qualifications. It lists preparers who hold professional credentials the IRS recognizes, such as CPAs, attorneys and enrolled agents, and preparers who completed the IRS Annual Filing Season Program. A preparer with only a PTIN will not appear on it, which is not wrongdoing on its own, but it does mean you should rely on other checks, such as references, years in practice and a clear fee quote.

Check state and professional records

CPAs and attorneys are licensed by their state, and you can verify a license on the state board or bar association site. Ask whether the preparer carries professional liability coverage and whether they will represent you if you get a notice. In my own work I tell clients up front what I am credentialed to do and what I am not. For example, I am a CPA candidate, not a CPA, and I say so.

Questions worth asking

  1. What is your PTIN and what credentials do you hold?
  2. How are your fees set, and can I have them in writing?
  3. Will you sign the return and give me a complete copy?
  4. Will you be available if I get an IRS or state letter later?
  5. How do you protect my documents and Social Security number?

The same habits good preparers have are worth knowing as a customer. I outlined them in what a tax preparer checks before signing your return, including how to read the summary pages before you approve anything.

If you already used a preparer you now doubt

Do not panic, and do not ignore it. Pull your copy of the return and compare it to your documents. If something looks wrong, an amended return may fix it. If the preparer committed misconduct, use Form 14157, Complaint: Tax Return Preparer. If the preparer filed or changed your return without your consent, also use Form 14157-A, Tax Return Preparer Fraud or Misconduct Affidavit. If your identity may have been misused, ask about Form 14039, Identity Theft Affidavit. Keep every message and receipt. If you later get a letter from the IRS, decoding an IRS notice will help you figure out what it is and how fast to respond.

The short version

Real preparers sign, show their PTIN, charge by the work, and give you a copy. If any of those is missing, keep your documents and find someone else. You can read about how I work with clients on my about page.

Frequently asked questions

What is a ghost tax preparer?

A ghost preparer prepares a return for pay but will not sign it or list a PTIN, as the IRS requires. The taxpayer is left responsible for anything on the return.

How do I verify a preparer’s PTIN or credentials?

Ask for the PTIN, which begins with P followed by eight digits. Look up preparers with IRS-recognized credentials, such as CPAs, attorneys and enrolled agents, and Annual Filing Season Program participants in the IRS preparer directory, and check state boards for licenses. A PTIN-only preparer will not appear in the directory. The IRS notes that many of these preparers still do quality work, so absence from the directory is not by itself a red flag, but you should choose carefully.

Where do I report a bad tax preparer?

Use Form 14157, Complaint: Tax Return Preparer. If the preparer filed or changed your return without your consent, also file Form 14157-A, Tax Return Preparer Fraud or Misconduct Affidavit, with the IRS.
