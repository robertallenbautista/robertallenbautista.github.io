First-Time Landlord or Short-Term Rental Host: Schedule E or Schedule C, Depreciation Basics and Records

Sorting out the schedule and the records early saves cleanup later.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator, CPA candidate · Published 2026-11-25

Key takeaways

  • The IRS says you can generally use Schedule E for real estate rental income and expenses. If you provide substantial services mainly for your tenant's convenience, you report on Schedule C instead.
  • Under the passive activity rules, an activity is generally not treated as a rental activity if the average period of customer use is seven days or less.
  • According to IRS Publication 527, residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years. Land is not depreciable, so you split the purchase price between land and building.
  • Start your records the month you buy or begin renting, and keep a log of each stay if you run a short-term rental.

If you started renting out a house, a duplex, a basement apartment or a spare property this year, your tax return gets a new piece. The schedule that income goes on, and whether the rental counts as a business for some purposes, depends on how you run it. Sorting that out early saves cleanup later.

This article covers the basics for a first-year landlord or a short-term rental host in the Kansas City area, on a 2026 return filed in 2027.

Schedule E is the usual home for rental income

The IRS says you can generally use Schedule E for real estate rental income and expenses. Rent you collect, in cash or in the fair market value of property or services you receive, is taxable rental income. You subtract the expenses of owning and running the rental and report the result.

The IRS also says rental expenses are deductible if they are incurred for the production of income, or for managing, conserving or maintaining property held to produce income. That covers things like repairs, property management fees, insurance, utilities you pay for the rental, and mortgage interest on the rental property, subject to the details in the rules.

When Schedule C comes into play

The IRS says that if you provide substantial services mainly for your tenant's convenience, you report on Schedule C instead. Compare renting out an apartment with running something closer to a hotel. Providing a roof and basic upkeep falls on one side. Daily cleaning, meals or concierge-style help falls on the other.

Short-term rentals sit in a gray area, and the answer turns on the services you offer and the length of stay. A Chief Counsel memorandum and IRS Publication 925 both discuss an exception under the passive activity rules. An activity is generally not treated as a rental activity if the average period of customer use is seven days or less, and it can also fall outside the rental definition if the average is 30 days or less and you provide significant personal services. You figure the average by dividing the total days in all rental periods by the number of rentals in the year. Routine cleaning of common areas and repairs do not count as significant personal services under that publication.

The schedule you use changes which other rules apply to the income, so have a preparer look at your actual facts. A weekend Airbnb listing with a self-service lockbox is different from a property where you do the cleaning and breakfast.

Depreciation, in plain terms

A rental building wears out on paper even when it holds up in real life. The tax rules let you deduct a slice of the building's cost each year.

According to IRS Publication 527, residential rental property is depreciated over 27.5 years under the usual method, using straight-line depreciation and a mid-month convention. The property has to earn 80% or more of its gross rental income from dwelling units to qualify as residential rental property. Land is not depreciable, because the IRS says it does not wear out or become obsolete. So you split your purchase price between land and building and depreciate only the building.

Depreciation starts when the property is placed in service, which means ready and available for rent. The IRS says to report it on Form 4562.

Here is a made-up example. Suppose you buy a rental for $200,000 and the building portion is $160,000. Divide $160,000 by 27.5 years and the full-year depreciation is about $5,818. The first year is smaller because of the mid-month convention. The numbers are invented to show the method. Your split between land and building will come from your own paperwork, such as a closing statement or a county assessment.

The first-year problem: personal use

Many first-time hosts also use the place themselves. The rules change when you use the property as a home. The IRS says that if you use a property as your home and rent it for fewer than 15 days during the year, you do not include the rent in income, and the related expenses are not rental expenses. Once you rent for more days, the personal-use rules become more involved, and you may need to divide expenses between personal and rental use. IRS Topic 415 covers those rules.

If you rent part of your own home, such as a room or a basement unit, you generally split costs between the rental part and the personal part. Keep the square footage numbers you used.

Losses and other taxes

If you do not use the property as a home and you rent it to make a profit, the IRS notes that losses may be limited and sends you to Publication 925 on passive activity rules. Rental profit may also be subject to the net investment income tax. Ask your preparer whether either rule touches your return, because both depend on your total income.

Records to keep from day one

Start these records the month you buy or begin renting:

  • The closing statement from the purchase, which shows the price, fees and sometimes the land value.
  • The date the property was ready to rent, and the date of the first tenant or guest.
  • Every rent payment received, with date and source, and any security deposits (kept separate from income unless you keep them).
  • Receipts for repairs, supplies, utilities, insurance and property management.
  • Mortgage interest and property tax statements.
  • Separate records for improvements, such as a new roof, because the IRS says you need the improvement costs when you sell or depreciate.
  • For short-term rentals, a log of each stay with check-in and check-out dates, so you can figure the average length.
  • Mileage logs for trips to the property, if you drive for repairs or showings.

A separate bank account for the rental makes this easier.

Kansas, Missouri and your city

State and local rules sit on top of the federal ones. Kansas and Missouri each have their own income tax filing rules, and a rental on one side of State Line Road while you live on the other side raises questions about where to file. Cities and counties may also have licensing, permit or lodging tax rules for rentals, especially short-term ones. This article does not cover those, and the details vary. Check with your city or county office and the Kansas or Missouri Department of Revenue, or ask your preparer.

Questions to ask your preparer

  • Should my rental go on Schedule E or Schedule C, based on the services I provide and my average stay?
  • How should I split the purchase price between land and building?
  • When was my property placed in service for depreciation?
  • Do passive loss limits or the net investment income tax apply to me?
  • Where do I owe state or local tax on this rental if I live in a different state?
