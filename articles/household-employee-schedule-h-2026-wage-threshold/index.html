Hiring a Nanny, Caregiver or House Cleaner: Schedule H and the 2026 Wage Threshold

Many families learn they are household employers only when they see a form they have never filled out.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator · Published 2026-11-27

Key takeaways

  • For 2026, IRS Publication 926 says Social Security and Medicare taxes apply to a household worker you pay $3,000 or more in cash wages in 2026. The 2025 figure was $2,800.
  • The IRS says the employee's share is 7.65% of cash wages, and you owe a matching 7.65% as the employer.
  • Most household employers report these taxes on Schedule H, which goes with your Form 1040, due April 15, 2027.
  • If you pay a household employee wages subject to these taxes, you generally give the worker a Form W-2. The date for 2026 is February 1, 2027.

If you pay someone to work in or around your home, you may be a household employer. That can apply to a nanny, a home-care aide for a parent, a house cleaner who comes every week, or a private cook. Many families do not realize it until they see a tax form they have never filled out. This article explains the 2026 threshold, how the tax works, and what to set up before the year ends.

Who counts as a household employee

A household employee generally does work in or around your home, and you control what work gets done and how. The IRS publishes Publication 926, Household Employer's Tax Guide, to help sort out who is an employee and who is an independent contractor. A cleaning company that sends its own crew and sets its own methods is different from a person you hire directly and direct yourself. If you are not sure which one you have, read Publication 926 or ask your preparer, because the answer drives everything else.

The 2026 threshold

For 2026, Publication 926 says Social Security and Medicare taxes apply to a household worker you pay $3,000 or more in cash wages in 2026. IRS Topic 756 gives the same $3,000 figure and notes that the threshold can change from year to year. For comparison, the 2025 figure was $2,800, so the number has moved up.

The test counts cash wages. It looks at the cash you pay during the calendar year. Once you cross the threshold, the taxes apply to the wages. Ask your preparer how the rule handles a worker who is paid below the threshold in one year and above it in the next.

The taxes involved

The IRS says the employee's share is 7.65% of cash wages, and you owe a matching 7.65% as the employer. The employee's share is 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare. You are generally the one who withholds the employee share from pay, and then you pay both shares. There is no wage base limit for Medicare tax.

Federal unemployment tax, known as FUTA, is separate. If you paid cash wages to household employees totaling more than $1,000 in any calendar quarter in the current or prior year, you generally owe FUTA on the first $7,000 paid to each household employee. Publication 926 also lists exceptions for certain family members, so if your caregiver is a relative, check the rules before you assume anything.

Income tax withholding from the worker's pay is a separate question from Social Security and Medicare. Publication 926 explains how it works, and your preparer can walk you through it.

Where Schedule H fits

Instead of a separate payroll return, most household employers report these taxes on Schedule H, Household Employment Taxes, which goes with your Form 1040. The IRS says to use Schedule H when the cash wages you paid were subject to Social Security, Medicare or federal unemployment tax. For 2026 wages, you file it with your return, which is due April 15, 2027, a Thursday.

The tax from Schedule H is added to the tax on your Form 1040. So the household employment tax becomes part of your final balance due or refund. If your withholding at your own job is low, you may owe more than you expect. Some employers raise their W-4 withholding to cover the bill, and some make estimated payments. Talk to your preparer about which method fits.

The W-2 for your employee

If you pay a household employee wages that are subject to these taxes, you generally give them a Form W-2 after the year ends. Topic 154 points to the same date as other W-2 forms for 2026: February 1, 2027, because January 31 falls on a Sunday. You file the W-2 and W-3 with the Social Security Administration as well. The W-2 is the document the worker uses to file their own return and to build Social Security credit.

So collect the worker's name and Social Security number exactly as they appear on the card when you hire them. Publication 926 lists the other forms a household employer needs, and your preparer can help if you have never used them.

A made-up example

This is a made-up example. A family hires a cleaner who comes every other week and pays her $150 each visit. Across 26 visits she earns $3,900 in the year. That is above the $3,000 threshold, so the family owes the Social Security and Medicare taxes on all of her wages, 7.65% from them as the employer and 7.65% from her, which they may withhold from her pay. If the same family paid a neighbor's teenager $400 for babysitting, that would be well below the threshold and would not trigger the tax on that worker, subject to the family-member and age rules in Publication 926. These numbers are invented, and your own facts decide the outcome.

Records to keep

Start these records the first week the person works for you.

  • The worker's full legal name and Social Security number.
  • Any hiring forms your preparer or Publication 926 says you need.
  • A pay log showing date, hours, rate, amount paid and any amount withheld.
  • Copies of each payment, whether it was a check or an electronic transfer.
  • Any paid-time-off and holiday policy you agreed on.

Paying someone in cash with no records invites problems. A pay log with dates and amounts is simple to keep, and you will want it when it is time to fill out Schedule H.

Why this gets missed

Many people assume the house cleaner or the nanny is a business owner who handles their own taxes. Sometimes that assumption is right. If the person runs their own cleaning business, brings their own crew and equipment and serves several clients, they may be an independent contractor. If you hire an individual and direct the work, the household employer rules are more likely to apply. When the facts are not clear, asking early is easier than correcting years of returns later.

Questions to ask your preparer

  • Is my worker a household employee or an independent contractor?
  • Did I cross the $3,000 threshold in 2026, and when?
  • Do I owe FUTA, and does a family-member exception apply?
  • Should I withhold from the worker's pay, and should I adjust my own withholding?
  • What should I do if I paid someone in earlier years and never reported it?
