Hobby or Business? How the IRS Decides and What It Means on Your Tax Return

Selling crafts, photos or resale finds on the side? The line between a hobby and a business changes how your income is taxed.

By Robert Allen Bautista, CPA candidate · Kansas City tax educator · Published 2026-11-23

Key takeaways

  • The IRS looks at whether you carry on the activity to make a profit, using nine factors rather than one test.
  • Hobby income is still taxable and is reported on your return.
  • Business income and expenses go on Schedule C, and net earnings may be subject to self-employment tax.
  • The IRS says that in general an activity that makes a profit in three of five years is presumed to be a business.

Calling your side activity a hobby does not make the income tax-free. A lot of Kansas City neighbors have one: baking, woodworking, photography, reselling thrift finds, a small online shop. Then a form arrives or someone mentions “business” and the question comes up. Is this a hobby or a business, and does it change my taxes? The answer is yes, in several ways.

Both are taxable

First, the part people get wrong in both directions. The IRS says income from a hobby is generally still income and must be reported. Calling it a hobby does not make it tax-free, and it does not mean you can skip reporting because the money is small. The question is not whether the income is taxed. It is how.

What the IRS looks at

The IRS looks at whether you carry on the activity with the intent to make a profit. There is no single test. The agency lists nine factors it considers, which I paraphrase here:

  1. Whether you carry on the activity in a businesslike way, with accurate books and records.
  2. Whether the time and effort you put in show intent to make a profit.
  3. Whether you depend on the income for your livelihood.
  4. Whether your losses come from circumstances beyond your control or are normal in the start-up phase.
  5. Whether you change your methods to improve profitability.
  6. Whether you or your advisors have the knowledge needed to run it as a business.
  7. Whether you succeeded in making a profit in similar activities in the past.
  8. Whether the activity makes a profit in some years and how much.
  9. Whether you can expect to make a future profit from the appreciation of the assets used.

The IRS also notes that the elements of personal pleasure or recreation can be considered, but the fact that you enjoy the work does not by itself make it a hobby. No one factor decides it, and the IRS looks at all the facts.

The three-of-five-years presumption

The IRS says an activity is presumed to be carried on for profit if it makes a profit in at least three of the last five tax years, with a different period for activities involving breeding, training, showing or racing horses. This is a presumption, not a guarantee, and it does not mean a loss year automatically makes you a hobby. If you are unsure and just starting, you can ask about Form 5213, which lets you postpone the IRS decision for a period. Read the instructions on irs.gov first, since the election has timing rules.

How each is reported

Hobby. You report the income as other income on your return. In recent years the IRS has said hobby expenses cannot be deducted, so you generally cannot subtract your costs against that income. Check the current-year IRS guidance before you file, because this rule was set by recent tax law and applies by year.

Business. You report income and expenses on Schedule C. Your net profit flows to your return and, if it is above the IRS threshold, to Schedule SE for self-employment tax, which covers Social Security and Medicare. Because no employer withholds anything, you may also need to make quarterly estimated tax payments. My piece on why your first freelance tax bill was so big explains how that bill adds up.

Payment apps and 1099-K

If you sell through an app or marketplace, you may receive Form 1099-K. The form reports payments, not profit, and the IRS has changed the reporting threshold in recent years, so check the current rule at irs.gov. Receiving or not receiving a 1099-K does not decide whether you have a hobby or a business. My article on your first 1099-K walks through what to do with it, including selling personal items at a loss.

Why the label matters for your tax bill

  • A business can deduct ordinary and necessary expenses against income. A hobby generally cannot.
  • A business owes self-employment tax on net earnings, while hobby income generally does not.
  • A business may carry a loss against other income within IRS limits. A hobby loss is not deductible.
  • A business needs better records, such as receipts, mileage and a record of sales.

The business label is not automatically better. If your costs are small and you do the work for fun, hobby treatment can be simpler. If your costs are high and you intend to profit, treating it as a business and keeping records usually makes more sense.

Local items to check

Depending on what you sell, you may need to look at sales tax registration and local licenses. Missouri and Kansas each have rules, so check dor.mo.gov and ksrevenue.gov and your city for current requirements. Kansas City, Missouri has its own earnings tax rules that can apply to self-employed income, and my article on Kansas City earnings tax scenarios covers the main cases.

What to do this week if you are in the gray zone

  • Write down the nine factors and note honestly how your activity fares on each.
  • Open a separate checking account and start tracking income and expenses.
  • Save receipts, mileage and dates.
  • Decide how you will price and promote, so the activity looks like a plan rather than a pastime.
  • Ask a qualified tax preparer if you are close to the line.

Frequently asked questions

Is hobby income taxable?

Generally yes. The IRS says hobby income must be reported on your return, though how you report it differs from business income.

How does the IRS tell a hobby from a business?

It looks at whether you operate to make a profit, using nine factors, including how businesslike you are, your time and effort, and your history of profit or loss.

What is the three-of-five-year rule?

The IRS says an activity is generally presumed to be for profit if it makes a profit in at least three of five consecutive years. It is a presumption, not a guarantee.
