The S Corp Break-Even Math: When an Election Actually Saves You Money

Everyone says an S corp saves self-employment tax. Sometimes it does, and sometimes it costs more than it saves. Here is how to run the numbers yourself.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • An S corp election can reduce self-employment tax, but only on profit above a reasonable salary for the work you do.
  • The savings must be larger than the new costs: payroll service, a separate business return, state fees and unemployment tax on your own wages.
  • At lower profit levels the math often fails. Many owners find the break-even point is well above their current income.
  • An LLC is a legal structure and an S corp is a tax election. You can have an LLC taxed as an S corp, but one does not require the other.
  • Run the comparison with your real numbers before filing anything, and check IRS.gov and your state for current rules and fees.

The pitch for an S corporation is simple and appealing: stop paying self-employment tax on all of your profit. What the pitch usually skips is the arithmetic on the other side of the ledger. After years of reviewing small business returns, I treat the decision as a break-even problem. You are comparing a known saving against a known list of new costs, and the answer depends on your profit more than on anything else.

What actually changes with the election

A sole proprietor or single-member LLC pays self-employment tax of 15.3% on net earnings (the Social Security and Medicare pieces, subject to the yearly Social Security wage base). If you elect S corporation status, the business files its own return, and you become an employee of your own company. Part of the profit is paid to you as wages, which carry payroll tax. The rest is distributed to you as profit, which is not subject to self-employment or payroll tax. Income tax still applies to all of it.

The savings come from that second bucket. The catch is that the first bucket cannot be set to zero.

Step 1: Estimate a reasonable salary

The IRS expects an owner who works in the business to be paid reasonable compensation for the services performed before taking distributions. There is no formula. People look at what the business would pay someone else to do the same job, your hours, your training and what comparable roles pay in the Kansas City market. A salary of a few thousand dollars on a profitable consulting business is the classic red flag. I would rather see a defensible number written down, with the reasoning, than a clever low one.

Step 2: List the new costs

  • Payroll. You need to run real payroll with withholding and deposits. Payroll services typically charge a monthly fee, and you pay the employer share of payroll tax on your own wages.
  • A separate federal return. The business files Form 1120-S and issues you a Schedule K-1. Preparation fees are higher than for a Schedule C.
  • State unemployment tax. Your own wages may be subject to state unemployment tax. Rates and wage bases vary, so check with the Missouri or Kansas agency that applies to you.
  • State and local fees. Entity registration and annual filing costs differ by state. Check the current fee schedule with your Secretary of State.
  • Time. Keeping owner pay, distributions and books clean is real work. See my notes on bookkeeping software or a bookkeeper for how to keep that manageable.

Step 3: Work an example (hypothetical)

The figures below are illustrative only. They are not a prediction, and they simplify several things.

Imagine two consultants. Maya nets $100,000 a year. Dan nets $40,000.

Maya, $100,000 profit

  • As a sole proprietor: self-employment tax is 15.3% of 92.35% of profit, about $14,100.
  • As an S corp paying herself a reasonable $60,000: payroll tax is 15.3% of $60,000 (employee and employer halves together), about $9,200.
  • Gross difference: roughly $4,900.
  • Suppose payroll service, the extra return and unemployment tax add up to $2,500 to $3,500. The net annual saving is somewhere around $1,400 to $2,400, and it grows as profit rises.

Dan, $40,000 profit

  • Sole proprietor: self-employment tax is about $5,650.
  • S corp paying a reasonable $30,000: payroll tax is about $4,600.
  • Gross difference: about $1,050, which the same compliance costs wipe out. Dan would come out behind.

Two further effects belong in any real comparison. First, wages paid to you reduce the profit that qualifies for the qualified business income deduction, which can shrink the benefit. Second, the sole proprietor deduction for half of self-employment tax and the treatment of the employer half of payroll tax work differently, so the true saving is slightly different from the headline figure. That is why I run both versions on the actual return rather than trusting a rule of thumb.

Other factors that tilt the decision

  • Stability of profit. A salary is paid every year, even in a slow one. Volatile income makes the commitment riskier.
  • Retirement contributions. Plan limits for owner-employees are tied to wages, so a lower salary can lower what you can contribute.
  • Loans and benefits. A steady W-2 salary can matter when you apply for a mortgage.
  • Timing. There are deadlines for making the election, and late relief exists in some cases. Check IRS.gov for the current rules on Form 2553.

A simple decision test

Take your expected net profit for the year. Subtract a reasonable salary. Multiply the remainder by roughly 15%. That is a rough upper bound on the payroll-tax saving, before the QBI and income tax effects. If that number is not comfortably larger than your added annual costs, wait. Frequently asked questions

Is an LLC the same thing as an S corp?

No. An LLC is a legal entity created under state law. An S corporation is a federal tax status that an LLC or corporation can elect. An LLC taxed by default as a sole proprietorship is not an S corp.

How much salary is reasonable?

There is no fixed percentage. It is generally what the business would pay someone else to do the work you do, based on your duties, hours, experience and local pay for comparable roles. Document how you got to the figure.

At what profit level does an S election start to pay off?

It depends on your reasonable salary and your added costs. As a rough idea, owners often need net profit well above their reasonable salary before the saving outweighs payroll, return preparation and state costs. Run the numbers for your situation.

Can I take no salary and only distributions?

The IRS expects reasonable pay for services before distributions, and avoiding salary is a common audit issue. Do not plan around it.

Can I switch back to a sole proprietorship later?

You can revoke an S election, but there are timing rules and a waiting period before re-electing in many cases. Treat the election as a multi-year decision and check IRS.gov for current guidance.
