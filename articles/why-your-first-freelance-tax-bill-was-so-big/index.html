Why Your First Freelance Tax Bill Was So Big (and How to Plan for the Next One)

If your first freelance tax bill felt twice what you expected, you are not alone. Two taxes were stacked, and only one of them was on your radar.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • Self-employment tax is 15.3% on most net earnings: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. It is in addition to income tax.
  • As an employee you paid half through your paycheck and your employer paid half. As a freelancer you owe both halves.
  • You owe it once net self-employment earnings reach $400, even if your income tax is low or zero.
  • You can deduct half of the self-employment tax when calculating income tax, which softens the stack a little.
  • Setting aside a percentage of every payment, and paying estimates, prevents the surprise.

Imagine a new freelancer who expects a tax bill of a few thousand dollars, then sees a total on the return that is considerably higher. In a case like this, the cause is very often the same: they planned for income tax and forgot self-employment tax.

What self-employment tax is

When you work for an employer, Social Security and Medicare taxes are split: 6.2% and 1.45% come out of your paycheck, and your employer pays a matching 6.2% and 1.45%. That makes 15.3% in total, but you only see half of it. When you are self-employed, you are the employer too. You pay both halves, and the form that figures it is Schedule SE.

  • 12.4% for Social Security, up to a yearly wage base that changes. Check IRS.gov for the current figure.
  • 2.9% for Medicare, with no cap.
  • An additional 0.9% Medicare tax can apply at higher income levels.

The 92.35% detail

The tax is not charged on your full net profit. It is applied to 92.35% of it, which mirrors the fact that employers get a deduction for their half. So the real self-employment tax rate on your profit is closer to 14.1%.

A worked example (hypothetical)

Illustrative numbers only. Suppose Priya, a freelance designer in Kansas City, has $50,000 of net profit after expenses.

  1. Multiply $50,000 by 92.35%: $46,175.
  2. Multiply by 15.3%: about $7,065 of self-employment tax.
  3. Half of that, about $3,532, is deductible when figuring income tax.
  4. Her income tax is then figured on $50,000 less the half deduction, less the standard deduction and any other items such as the qualified business income deduction.

The two taxes sit side by side. Income tax depends on brackets and deductions, and it can be modest. Self-employment tax begins with the first dollar of profit above the threshold, with no standard deduction to shield it. That is why a freelancer with a low income tax can still owe a four-figure bill.

The $400 threshold

You owe self-employment tax if your net earnings from self-employment were $400 or more for the year. The threshold is on net earnings, not on your gross receipts. Even if your income is low enough that you owe no income tax, the $400 rule can still require a Schedule SE. This often catches part-time gig workers and sellers, a group I wrote about in my first 1099-K guide.

What it does and does not cover

This tax is how you earn Social Security credits and Medicare coverage, so it is not wasted. It does not cover income tax, state tax or any local earnings tax such as the Kansas City, Missouri 1% earnings tax. Those are separate. If you have a W-2 job as well, the Social Security part is limited by the wage base across both, but the Medicare part is not.

Ways to plan

Set aside a percentage from every payment

Many new freelancers do well with 25% to 30% of net profit as a starting rule. The right figure depends on your bracket, your state and your deductions. Move it to a separate account as soon as a client pays.

Make estimated payments

The IRS expects payments during the year. My month-by-month estimated tax calendar for a first-year freelancer shows what to pay and when, including how the prior-year safe harbor works. The 2027 estimated tax dates are in a separate guide.

Reduce net profit legitimately

Every ordinary and necessary business expense lowers both income tax and self-employment tax. Tracking mileage, software, supplies and a home office is worth the effort, and good records matter more than clever ideas. Good bookkeeping, covered in my guide to bookkeeping software or a bookkeeper, is the foundation.

Use retirement contributions and the health insurance deduction

A SEP IRA or Solo 401(k) contribution reduces income tax but does not reduce self-employment tax. The self-employed health insurance deduction also lowers income tax only. They are valuable, but they do not fix this part of the bill.

Revisit your structure when profit grows

At higher profit levels some owners elect S corporation treatment. It is not automatic and has costs, which I work through in a separate piece on the break-even math for an S corporation election.

Making next year predictable

Your first freelance bill combines income tax and self-employment tax, with no employer withholding either one. Track profit each month and pay estimates quarterly, and next year's bill shouldn't surprise you. If you would like help planning, you can find more about my work on the about me page.

Frequently asked questions

What is the self-employment tax rate?

15.3% on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings, made up of 12.4% Social Security (up to a yearly cap) and 2.9% Medicare. An extra 0.9% Medicare tax can apply at higher incomes.

Is self-employment tax deductible?

Half of it is deductible when you calculate income tax. The deduction reduces income tax but does not reduce the self-employment tax itself.

Do I owe self-employment tax on small amounts of income?

If your net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more for the year, yes. Below that, no self-employment tax is due.

Why do I owe even though I had low income?

Self-employment tax is figured on net profit and has no standard deduction, so it can be due even when income tax is small or zero.

How much should I set aside?

A common starting range is 25% to 30% of net profit. Your own number depends on income, deductions and state, so adjust after your first quarter or two.
