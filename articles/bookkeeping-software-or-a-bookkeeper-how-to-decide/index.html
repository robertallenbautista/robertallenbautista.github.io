Bookkeeping Software or a Bookkeeper? How Kansas City Small Businesses Can Decide

The software is not the bookkeeping. Here is how to tell which kind of help your business actually needs.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • Software records and organizes transactions. A bookkeeper makes sure they are categorized correctly and the books actually reconcile.
  • If you have a few dozen transactions a month and one bank account, software alone is often enough.
  • Hire help when books fall behind, when you have payroll, inventory or several accounts, or when you dread opening the app.
  • The middle path is common: you use the software, and a bookkeeper reviews and cleans up monthly or quarterly.
  • Whatever you choose, reconcile your accounts monthly. That one habit prevents most year-end disasters.

A common question from new business owners is, "Should I buy QuickBooks, or should I hire a bookkeeper?" It is a fair question, but it frames the choice a little wrong. Software and a bookkeeper are not two versions of the same thing. One is a tool. The other is a person who knows how to use the tool and, more importantly, knows what correct books look like. As someone who reviewed other people's accounting work for years, I know both approaches can work well, and both can fail.

What bookkeeping software does well

Modern accounting software connects to your bank and credit card accounts, imports transactions, lets you send invoices, tracks bills and produces reports such as a profit and loss statement. It is fast, inexpensive compared with hiring someone, and available at any hour. For a simple business, it can do nearly everything.

What software does not do is understand your business. It guesses categories from patterns. It may label a personal purchase as supplies, a loan payment as an expense or an owner deposit as income. Those guesses are the bookkeeping errors I find most often. The numbers look neat and are wrong.

What a bookkeeper adds

A good bookkeeper does the work behind the screen:

  • Categorizes transactions correctly and consistently
  • Reconciles each account to the bank statement, so the books tie to reality
  • Separates owner draws, loans and transfers from income and expenses
  • Tracks sales tax, payroll liabilities and 1099 contractors
  • Prepares clean reports for you and for your tax preparer

A bookkeeper is also an accountability partner. Many owners are not short on ability, only on time and routine. Someone expecting your receipts every month changes that.

A simple way to decide

Run through these questions honestly.

Software alone is probably fine if:

  • You have one business bank account and one credit card.
  • You have a few dozen transactions a month.
  • You are a sole proprietor or single-member LLC with no employees.
  • You will actually reconcile each month, even if it takes 30 minutes.

Add a bookkeeper if:

  • You have employees or run payroll.
  • You carry inventory or sell on several platforms.
  • You have multiple accounts, loans or business partners.
  • Your books are more than a month or two behind.
  • You avoid the software because it stresses you out.

The hybrid approach most owners end up with

In my experience the best arrangement for a small business is a hybrid. You pay for software and handle the daily items: sending invoices and snapping receipts. A bookkeeper or accountant logs in monthly or quarterly to review categories, reconcile accounts and fix anything strange. It costs far less than full-service bookkeeping, and it keeps the books honest. It also means that at tax time I receive a file I can use right away, not a project to rebuild.

Warning signs your books need help

  • The bank balance in the software does not match the actual bank balance.
  • A large number of transactions are sitting in an "uncategorized" or "ask my accountant" account.
  • You cannot say what your profit was last quarter.
  • Personal and business spending are mixed in the same account.
  • You are getting IRS or state notices about payroll or sales tax.

If two or more of those apply, you are no longer saving money by doing it alone. Cleaning up a full year of books costs far more than keeping them current. For the fundamentals, my checklist on starting your small business books covers what to set up in the first month, and it applies even if you already have software.

What to ask before hiring a bookkeeper

  1. What software do you work in, and who owns the file? It should be yours.
  2. Do you reconcile accounts every month?
  3. How do you handle the owner's personal spending and draws?
  4. Will you coordinate with my tax preparer at year-end?
  5. Is the fee fixed, hourly or by the number of transactions?

A bookkeeper is not a licensed tax professional by default, so ask about the scope. Many handle the books, and leave tax filing and advice to a preparer or CPA.

Why this matters at tax time

The tax return is only as good as the books under it. Missed deductions and miscategorized expenses lead directly to overpaying. If you do not know where to start, review the write-offs most small business owners miss. The amount you find will tend to be larger than the cost of tidy books. Keep the support for each entry too, and see what a preparer checks before signing your return to understand how clean books are reviewed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just do my own books if I use good software?

Yes, for a simple business, if you reconcile every month and categorize carefully. Problems start when books fall behind or when payroll, inventory or several accounts get involved.

Is a bookkeeper the same as an accountant?

No. A bookkeeper records and organizes transactions. An accountant analyzes the numbers, advises on tax and structure and may prepare returns. Many small businesses use both.

How often should books be reconciled?

Monthly. Reconciling means matching your records to your bank and credit card statements, and it is the best way to catch mistakes early.

What should I give my tax preparer from my books?

A profit and loss statement, a balance sheet, a trial balance or general ledger if available, and access to bank statements. Clean, reconciled books speed up the return.

What if my books are already a mess?

Ask for a cleanup, which means catching up transactions, reconciling accounts and fixing categories. Start with the most recent year and work backward as needed.
