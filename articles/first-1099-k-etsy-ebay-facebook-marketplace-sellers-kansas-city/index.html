Your First 1099-K from Etsy, eBay or Facebook Marketplace: A Kansas City Seller's Guide

A 1099-K shows up in your mailbox or inbox and the number looks scary. Here is what it really means, and what to do next.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • A 1099-K reports gross payments, not profit. Your taxable income is what is left after costs, fees and shipping.
  • Selling used personal items for less than you paid is generally not taxable. Reselling items for profit as a business is.
  • Selling as a business means Schedule C, and usually self-employment tax once net earnings reach $400.
  • If the form is wrong, ask the platform for a corrected one. Do not just ignore it, because the IRS gets a copy.
  • Keep your own sales records. They are what turns a big gross number into a small, accurate profit.

Every year around late January, sellers who have never thought of themselves as business owners get a form called 1099-K. It arrives from Etsy, eBay, PayPal, Venmo, Poshmark, a payment processor or a card reader. The number on it is often much larger than what the seller actually kept. Many sellers assume they will be taxed on the whole amount. They almost never are.

What a 1099-K actually reports

Form 1099-K is an information return. A payment company sends one copy to you and one to the IRS, listing the gross payments it processed for you during the year. Gross means before refunds, shipping you paid, platform fees, sales tax collected and the cost of what you sold. It is a starting point, not a tax bill.

The reporting threshold has changed several times in recent years, and Congress changed it again in 2025. Do not assume the number you remember from the news still applies. For 2026, check IRS.gov for the current threshold. The key point does not depend on the threshold: income is taxable whether or not you receive a form. If you earn a profit selling things, it belongs on your return even if the platform never sent you anything.

Hobby, business or just clearing out the garage?

Your first job is to sort what you sold into two piles.

Pile one: personal items you sold used

Selling a couch, a bike or old video games on Facebook Marketplace is usually not a business. If you sold the item for less than you originally paid, you generally have no taxable gain. You also cannot deduct the loss on personal-use property. If you sold something for more than you paid, such as a collectible that went up in value, the gain is generally taxable, usually as a capital gain.

Pile two: things you bought or made to resell

If you make jewelry for Etsy, flip thrift-store finds on eBay or buy wholesale lots to resell, you are in business in the eyes of the IRS. The test is whether you do it regularly, with the intent to make a profit. That income and the related expenses go on Schedule C, attached to your Form 1040.

The reason the sorting matters is simple. If you put everything on Schedule C, you may pay self-employment tax on money that was never income. If you ignore pile two, you may owe tax and penalties later. A good record of what each sale was is what keeps this clean.

How to turn a big 1099-K into an accurate profit

Start with the gross amount on the form, then work down:

  • Cost of goods sold: what you paid for inventory or materials for the items you sold.
  • Platform and payment fees: listing fees, final value fees and processing fees. These are often buried in monthly statements, so download them.
  • Shipping and packaging: postage, boxes, labels and tape.
  • Refunds and returns: money you sent back to customers.
  • Other costs: supplies, software, advertising, a portion of mileage for supply runs and, if you qualify, a home office.

What is left is net profit. That number, not the 1099-K, drives your tax. For more on those last two items, see my guides to the home office deduction and to standard mileage versus actual expenses.

Self-employment tax: the part that surprises people

When you are in business for yourself, you owe self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare) in addition to regular income tax. The combined rate is 15.3%, applied to a slightly reduced amount of your net profit, and it applies once your net self-employment earnings reach $400 for the year. Many first-time sellers are caught off guard because nothing was withheld from their sales. If you expect to owe, read about quarterly estimated taxes and the safe harbor rule, so a larger April bill does not become an underpayment penalty too.

What if the 1099-K is wrong?

Errors happen. Common ones are a form that includes money sent by friends and family, a form that double counts a transfer, or a form that reports payments for personal items you sold at a loss. Do this:

  1. Contact the platform that issued the form and ask for a corrected 1099-K.
  2. If you cannot get a correction in time, do not ignore it. Report the amount as the form shows it and then use the appropriate adjustment on your return to remove the personal or erroneous portion, with a clear explanation. The IRS matches forms to returns, so a missing 1099-K invites a notice.
  3. Keep the evidence: statements, receipts and a short note on what the money was for.

The Kansas City angle

Missouri and Kansas both tax income, and you generally pay state income tax to the state where you live. If you live and sell from the Missouri side, your net profit also goes onto your Missouri return. The Kansas City earnings tax can matter too for self-employed people with business activity in the city, and I cover it in my article on the Kansas City 1% earnings tax.

Sales tax is a separate issue. Missouri and Kansas both have rules under which large marketplaces collect sales tax on your behalf for sales made through their platforms. Sales through your own website, at craft fairs or in person can work differently, so check with the Missouri Department of Revenue or the Kansas Department of Revenue if you sell outside a big marketplace.

Set up a simple system now

You do not need fancy software. Open a separate checking account or at least a separate payment-app account for selling. Keep a spreadsheet with a row per sale or per month. Save receipts for inventory the day you buy it. Frequently asked questions

Do I owe tax on the full amount shown on my 1099-K?

No. The form shows gross payments. You are taxed on net profit after the cost of what you sold, fees, shipping and other business costs, and generally not on personal items you sold for less than you paid.

What if I did not receive a 1099-K at all?

You still report your income. A form is not required for a sale to be taxable. Use your own sales records, bank deposits and platform reports to calculate your profit.

Do I need an LLC to sell on Etsy or eBay?

No. Most small sellers operate as sole proprietors and file Schedule C. An LLC is a separate decision about liability and structure, not a tax requirement.

Can I deduct the loss when I sell personal items for less than I paid?

Generally no. Losses on personal-use property are not deductible. They are also generally not taxable gains, so the sale usually has no tax effect.

Should I make estimated tax payments as an online seller?

Often yes, if you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding and credits. Check IRS.gov for the current rules and safe harbor amounts for 2026.
