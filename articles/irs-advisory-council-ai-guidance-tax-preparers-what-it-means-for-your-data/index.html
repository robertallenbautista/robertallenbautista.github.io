IRS Advisory Council Wants Updated AI Guidance: What It Means for Your Tax Data

The council says tax rules were not built for AI tools. The real risk is not a wrong answer. It is where your data goes.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax educator · Published 2026-11-19

Key takeaways

  • The IRS Advisory Council (IRSAC), a 37-member council, says existing standards on preparer conduct and taxpayer information were not developed with AI-assisted tools in mind.
  • Its annual report has 33 proposals. The AI ones ask the IRS to seek public comment on its Office of Professional Responsibility AI guidance and then reissue it.
  • IRSAC wants AI guidance to reach all PTIN holders, not only Circular 230 practitioners.
  • This is a recommendation, not an IRS rule, and the article reporting it includes no formal IRS response.
  • The practical risk is where client data goes, so ask your preparer whether they use AI and what they put into it.

When people worry about AI in tax prep, they usually picture a chatbot giving a wrong answer. That is a real problem, but it is the smaller one. A wrong answer can be caught in review. Data that has already been pasted into a tool you do not control is much harder to take back. A new recommendation to the IRS points at exactly that gap.

What the council said

On November 19, 2026, The Tax Adviser reported on the annual report of the IRS Advisory Council. IRSAC is a 37-member council, and the report released this week contains 33 proposals. Several of them concern artificial intelligence.

The council's core point is that existing standards on preparer conduct and taxpayer information “were not developed with modern AI-assisted tools and workflows in mind.” In plain terms, the rules were written before these tools became part of daily work, and nobody has fully updated them.

The five AI recommendations

  1. Public comment, then reissue. The IRS should seek public comment on this year's Office of Professional Responsibility AI guidance, then reissue it.
  2. Wider reach. AI guidance should extend to all PTIN holders, not only Circular 230 practitioners.
  3. Little direct guidance. Practitioners increasingly use AI to receive, process, analyze, transmit and store taxpayer information, and there is little direct guidance on this.
  4. A knowledge gap. Taxpayers and preparers may not understand how AI providers receive, store, use or retain submitted data.
  5. A security gap. The IRS's security guidance has a gap on AI vendor selection, data handling, access controls and retention.

Notice what the last three have in common. None of them is about whether the AI is smart enough. They are about what happens to taxpayer information once it enters the tool: who receives it, where it is stored, who can access it and how long it stays.

A recommendation, not a rule

It is worth being precise here. IRSAC advises the IRS. Its report is a set of recommendations, and the article describing it does not include a formal IRS response. Nothing in it changes what you or your preparer must do today. What it does show is that a body advising the IRS sees a gap that many preparers may already be noticing in their own offices.

Why where the data goes matters more than a wrong answer

If an AI tool misreads a rule, a careful preparer checks the result against the IRS instructions and the return. The error gets caught or it does not, but the damage stays inside the return. When a Social Security number or a full set of tax documents is pasted into a public chatbot, the question is different. You can no longer say with confidence where that information lives or how long it is kept. That uncertainty is the point of the fourth recommendation above: taxpayers and preparers may not understand how providers receive, store, use or retain what is submitted.

Identity theft is the backdrop. The same report says identity-theft cases average more than 15 months to resolve, with roughly 387,000 unresolved. When fixing a stolen identity takes that long, not handing out the raw material in the first place is cheap insurance.

If you are a taxpayer: questions to ask your preparer

  • Do you use AI tools in preparing returns?
  • If yes, which tools, and what information goes into them?
  • Is my name, Social Security number or any document ever entered into a public chatbot?
  • Does the tool keep what is entered, and for how long?

A good preparer will have a straight answer. If the answer is vague, that tells you something too. Checking who prepares your return is part of the same habit, and my guides on ghost preparers and what a preparer checks before signing cover it.

What never belongs in a public chatbot

  • Social Security numbers, yours or anyone else's
  • A full tax return
  • W-2s and other source documents
  • Your Identity Protection PIN (see my guide to the IP PIN)

A simple rule is to use AI to think, not to store. Ask it to explain a concept, outline a question to bring to your preparer, or draft a checklist, using no real identifiers. Then keep the actual documents in the places you already control.

If you are a preparer: write an AI policy

You do not need to wait for the IRS to finish its guidance to put your own house in order. A one-page written policy is enough to start. It should answer three questions:

  1. Which tools? List the AI tools approved for use, and say that anything not on the list is off limits for client work.
  2. What data? Say what may be entered and what may not. Client identifiers and source documents are the obvious things to keep out unless the vendor has been vetted.
  3. What retention? Find out how each provider receives, stores, uses and retains submitted data, and write down what you learn.

Those three headings line up with the gap IRSAC describes in the IRS's security guidance: vendor selection, data handling, access controls and retention. Add who in the office can use which tool, and you have covered all four.

Other items in the report

AI is only part of the report. IRSAC also proposes a public identity-theft case tracker and an “EIN Protection PIN” for businesses, more work against ghost preparers, and a simpler Form W-4.

Frequently asked questions

Is this a new IRS rule on AI?

No. It is a recommendation from the IRS Advisory Council, and the report coverage includes no formal IRS response. Nothing changes for taxpayers or preparers because of it alone.

Can my preparer use AI on my return?

The council did not say preparers cannot. Its concern is that guidance has not caught up with how the tools handle taxpayer information. Ask which tools your preparer uses and what goes into them.

What should I never paste into a public chatbot?

Social Security numbers, a full return, W-2s and your IP PIN. Use AI to think through a question, not to store or process your documents.

What should an AI policy for a tax office cover?

Which tools are approved, what data may be entered, and how each provider retains submitted data. Add access controls so only the right people use each tool.
