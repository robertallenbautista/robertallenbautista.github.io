What a Tax Preparer Checks Before Signing Your Return: A Reviewer's Checklist

After reviewing hundreds of returns, I learned that most errors are caught by the same short list of checks. Here it is.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-15

Key takeaways

  • A good review starts with identity, filing status and dependents, because a mismatch there causes the most rejections.
  • Every number should tie back to a source document, and every carryover should tie back to last year's return.
  • Credits that pay cash, such as the EITC, require extra due-diligence questions from your preparer. That is a good sign, not a nuisance.
  • You can run a basic version of this review yourself in 15 minutes before you sign anything.
  • Your preparer must sign the return and include a PTIN. If one will not, find another.

Before I sign any return, it goes through a review. For several years I was the person who reviewed other preparers' work: six or more of them at a CPA firm, on returns of all sizes. Before that, I spent four years at PwC, where nothing left the building without a second set of eyes. That experience taught me that quality in tax preparation is mostly a checklist and a habit. This is the version I use, in the order I use it.

Step 1: Identity and basics

It sounds trivial, and it is where a surprising share of problems begin. I check that every name and Social Security number matches the card exactly, including spelling, hyphens and suffixes. A name that does not match Social Security Administration records gets a return rejected, or a credit denied later. I also confirm the address, the bank account and routing numbers for any refund, and the correct tax year on every form.

Step 2: Filing status and dependents

Filing status drives the standard deduction, the tax brackets and which credits are available. I ask real questions here: who lived in the home, for how long and who paid the household costs. Head of household, in particular, has specific tests, and a status picked only because it gives a bigger refund is one of the most common errors I saw in other people's work. For dependents, I confirm that two people are not claiming the same child, because the second return filed will usually be rejected.

Step 3: Income completeness

The IRS receives copies of W-2s, 1099s and many other forms, and its computers compare them to your return. So I tie out the return to every income document, then ask about income that has no form: cash work, side jobs, online sales, tips, rental income and unemployment. A common slip is a single missing 1099 that triggers a notice months later. If you sold items online, see my guide on your first 1099-K.

Step 4: Compare with last year

I put this year's return next to last year's and look for anything that changed a lot. A large jump or drop in income, a deduction that appeared or vanished, or a new state all deserve a question. The comparison also catches carryovers that were forgotten, such as:

  • Capital loss carryovers
  • Net operating losses
  • Passive activity losses that were suspended
  • Depreciation on assets placed in service in earlier years
  • Credit carryforwards

Missing a carryover is not a mistake that the IRS ever points out to you. You simply overpay, quietly.

Step 5: Reasonableness of deductions

For self-employed clients, I look at the Schedule C as an auditor would. Are the expense categories plausible for this type of work? Is there a car deduction without a mileage log? Is the home office deduction supported by an actual dedicated space? Are meals handled correctly? I also check that a business with real income is not showing a large loss year after year, because that raises hobby-loss questions. Good records help here, and I describe how long to keep them in how long to keep tax records.

Step 6: Credits and due diligence

The credits that put money in your pocket, the Earned Income Tax Credit, the Child Tax Credit and the education credits, are the ones the IRS scrutinizes most. A paid preparer has to ask additional questions and keep records to prove it was done, on a form the IRS calls the due diligence checklist. If your preparer seems to be asking more questions than you expected, that is the system working. It protects you from having a credit removed and a penalty added later.

Step 7: Math, forms and state returns

Software does the arithmetic, but I still trace the key lines: income to adjusted gross income, to taxable income, to tax, to payments, to refund or balance due. I check that the federal numbers carried correctly to the state returns. For Kansas City clients, that often means confirming which state taxes which income. If you live in one state and work in the other, see the state tax basics for Kansas and Missouri.

Step 8: The signature

A paid preparer must sign the return and include a Preparer Tax Identification Number (PTIN). Never accept a return from someone who will not sign it, and never sign a blank or incomplete return yourself. You are the one responsible for what is on it, so read the summary page before you e-file. Most preparers and tax software let you view a draft before it is transmitted. Use that.

A 15-minute self-check before you sign

  1. Are all names, Social Security numbers and bank details right?
  2. Does every income document you received appear on the return?
  3. Does the filing status and the list of dependents match your real situation?
  4. Is the refund or balance due roughly what you expected? If not, ask why.
  5. Is the return signed by the preparer, with a PTIN?

If any answer is unclear, ask the question before filing, not after. A returned call costs a few minutes. An amended return costs weeks. And if you are trying to decide what to gather first, my guide on what to bring to your tax appointment lists the documents that make every step above easier.

Frequently asked questions

What is a PTIN and why does it matter?

A PTIN is a Preparer Tax Identification Number issued by the IRS. Anyone who is paid to prepare a federal return must have one and put it on your return along with their signature.

Why does my preparer ask so many questions about my household?

Filing status, dependents and credits such as the EITC require specific facts, and paid preparers must document that they asked. The questions protect you from having a credit denied later.

What should I do if I notice a mistake after filing?

Contact your preparer and ask about filing an amended return, Form 1040-X. Do not wait for a notice, because fixing an error yourself is usually easier than responding to one.

Is it a red flag if a preparer promises a big refund before seeing my documents?

Yes. A refund depends on your actual income, withholding and credits. A preparer who guarantees a number before reviewing your paperwork, or who bases the fee on a percentage of the refund, is a warning sign.

Should I review my own return even if I pay a professional?

Yes. You are responsible for the information on it. Reading the summary page and checking names, numbers and income takes a few minutes.
