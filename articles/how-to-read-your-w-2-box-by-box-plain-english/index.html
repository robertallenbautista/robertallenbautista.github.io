How to Read Your W-2, Box by Box, in Plain English

Your W-2 is one page, but every box feeds a different line of your return. Here is what each one means and what is worth double-checking before you file.

By Robert Allen Bautista · Kansas City tax preparer and accountant · Published 2026-11-16

Key takeaways

  • Box 1 is your taxable wages, and it is usually lower than your actual pay because pre-tax deductions come out first.
  • Box 2 is federal income tax withheld; it is the number that becomes a payment toward your tax bill.
  • Boxes 3 and 5 are often higher than Box 1, and that is normal when you contribute to a 401(k).
  • Box 12 codes look cryptic but mostly describe retirement and health benefits. Not every code is taxable.
  • Check your name, Social Security number and state boxes before you file. Errors are fixed with a corrected W-2.

A common question every January is “Why doesn’t my W-2 match my paycheck?” The form is not hard, but it is dense. Form W-2, Wage and Tax Statement, is what your employer reports to you and to the Social Security Administration, which shares the information with the IRS. Employers must furnish it to you by January 31 (the next business day if that date lands on a weekend), so most arrive in late January.

The top of the form: who is who

Boxes a through f hold your Social Security number, your employer’s EIN, the employer’s name and address, and your name and address. Check these first. A transposed digit in your Social Security number can delay a refund or trigger a mismatch notice. If your name changed after marriage, make sure the name on the W-2 matches what the Social Security Administration has on file.

Boxes 1 and 2: the two numbers most people look for

Box 1, wages, tips, other compensation. This is the amount subject to federal income tax. It is usually lower than your gross pay because pre-tax items, such as traditional 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums paid through a cafeteria plan and HSA payroll contributions, are subtracted before it is calculated. It goes on line 1a of Form 1040.

Box 2, federal income tax withheld. This is the total your employer sent to the IRS on your behalf. It is a credit against your tax, not a refund. Whether you get money back depends on whether Box 2 is more or less than your final tax. If you have more than one job, add up Box 2 from every W-2.

Boxes 3 through 6: Social Security and Medicare

  • Box 3 is wages subject to Social Security tax. It stops at the yearly Social Security wage base, which the Social Security Administration updates each year. If you earned above it, Box 3 will be capped.
  • Box 4 is the Social Security tax withheld, 6.2% of Box 3.
  • Box 5 is wages subject to Medicare tax. It has no cap.
  • Box 6 is Medicare tax withheld, 1.45% of Box 5, plus any Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9%, which employers must withhold on wages over $200,000 in a calendar year.

Boxes 3 and 5 are often higher than Box 1. That surprises people, but it is normal: 401(k) deferrals reduce income tax wages, but they do not reduce Social Security and Medicare wages. It is unusual for Box 1 to be higher than Box 5, so ask your payroll department if you see that.

Boxes 7 and 8: tips

Box 7 shows Social Security tips you reported to your employer. Box 8 shows allocated tips, which an employer assigns to tipped employees in certain large food and beverage establishments when reported tips fall short of a set percentage. Allocated tips are not included in Box 1, but you generally have to report them as income using Form 4137. Recent law changes added a deduction for qualified tips; the IRS has been updating forms and instructions for it, so check the current Form 1040 instructions or ask your preparer how it applies to you.

Boxes 10 and 11: benefits you may not know about

Box 10 reports dependent care benefits, such as a dependent care flexible spending account. Amounts above the exclusion limit become taxable, and you report the benefit on Form 2441. Box 11 reports distributions from certain nonqualified deferred compensation plans, which is rare for most hourly or salaried employees.

Box 12: the codes

Box 12 has up to four entries, each a letter code and a dollar amount. Common ones:

  • D: elective deferrals to a traditional 401(k).
  • AA: Roth 401(k) contributions. These are after-tax and are included in Box 1.
  • E and G: elective deferrals to a 403(b) plan (E) and to a 457(b) plan (G, which can also include employer contributions).
  • W: employer contributions to your health savings account, including amounts you chose to contribute through a cafeteria plan. You report these on Form 8889.
  • DD: the total cost of your employer-sponsored health coverage. It is for information only and is not taxable.
  • C: the taxable cost of group-term life insurance over $50,000, already included in Boxes 1, 3 and 5.

The IRS lists every code in the General Instructions for Forms W-2 and W-3. If your code is not in this list, look it up there rather than guessing.

Box 13 and Box 14

Box 13 has three checkboxes. “Retirement plan” being checked can limit whether you can deduct a traditional IRA contribution, which is worth knowing at tax time. “Statutory employee” and “Third-party sick pay” are less common. Box 14 is a free-form box where employers list items such as union dues, state disability insurance or tuition reimbursements. It has no standard meaning, so ask your payroll department if you cannot tell what an entry is.

Boxes 15 through 20: state and local

These are the boxes Kansas City workers should read carefully. Box 15 is the state and employer state ID, Box 16 is state wages and Box 17 is state income tax withheld. Boxes 18 through 20 cover local wages, local tax withheld and the locality name. If you work in Kansas City, Missouri, your employer generally withholds the 1% earnings tax, and it may appear in the local boxes. If you live in Kansas but work in Missouri, or the reverse, you can end up with two states’ worth of information on one form. I cover how the earnings tax works in a few common situations in my guide to Kansas City earnings tax scenarios.

A short checklist before you file

  1. Confirm your name and Social Security number match your card.
  2. Add up Box 1 and Box 2 across all W-2s.
  3. Compare Box 4 to 6.2% of Box 3 and Box 6 to 1.45% of Box 5. Large differences suggest a payroll error.
  4. Check that your state and local boxes match where you actually worked and lived.
  5. If a W-2 is missing after January 31, ask your employer first. If you still do not have it by the end of February, the IRS says you can call it for help. A wage and income transcript can show what was reported, but it may not be complete until employers have filed. If the deadline is near, you can file using Form 4852 and your last pay stub, then amend if the real W-2 differs.

If a W-2 has an error, your employer should issue a corrected Form W-2c. Do not file with numbers you know are wrong, and do not just change the figures yourself. Frequently asked questions

Why is Box 1 lower than my gross pay?

Pre-tax deductions such as traditional 401(k) contributions, pre-tax health premiums and HSA payroll contributions are subtracted before Box 1 is calculated, so it shows only wages that are taxable for federal income tax.

Why are Boxes 3 and 5 higher than Box 1?

401(k) deferrals reduce your income tax wages but not your Social Security and Medicare wages. That is normal. Box 3 can be capped at the Social Security wage base.

What do I do if my W-2 is wrong or missing?

Ask your employer first. For errors, your employer issues a corrected Form W-2c. If a W-2 is still missing at the end of February, you can call the IRS for help. A wage and income transcript may show what was reported, but it may not be complete until your employer has filed.
